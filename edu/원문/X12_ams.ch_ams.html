X12. Data Converters and PLLs, Worked

Very few IP blocks are purely digital at their boundary. A SerDes has an analogue front end; a sensor interface has a converter; every synchronous block has a clock that came from a PLL. A digital designer does not have to design these, but does have to budget for them, and budgeting requires the same arithmetic the analogue designer uses. This part supplies it.

X12.1 The converter equations, and what they leave out

SNR, ENOB and the 6.02N+1.76 formula

Given. A receiver needs 11 effective bits on a 500 MHz input. The converter's own thermal and distortion contribution is 13 ENOB.

How this is got wrong. By subtracting decibels instead of powers. Noise contributions add in power, so budgets must be combined as 10−SNR/10 terms and only then converted back. Subtracting 11 from 13 in dB gives a meaningless number. The second error, subtler, is to forget that the clock buffer between the PLL and the sampler adds its own jitter, often more than the PLL, so a budget that spends the whole allowance on the PLL has already failed.

Time interleaving is where the digital team earns its place in an analogue block. Running M converters in round robin multiplies the sample rate by M and introduces three mismatches: offset, which appears as spurs at multiples of fs/M; gain, which appears as spurs around the signal; and timing skew, which appears as signal-dependent spurs that grow with input frequency. None is removable by better layout alone at modern speeds; all three are corrected by digital background calibration that estimates the mismatch from the data and applies a correction. That estimator is a piece of DSP with a convergence time, a lock detector and a failure mode, and it is specified, modelled and verified by the digital side. A SerDes receiver's ADC is time-interleaved 32 or 64 ways, and its calibration loops are a substantial part of the digital block's area.

X12.2 Phase-locked loops: the transfer functions that matter

Why a PLL is a lowpass for reference noise and a highpass for VCO noise
StepJustification
1Open loop: G(s) = KpdF(s)Kvco/s, divided by N.Phase detector gain, loop filter, VCO as a phase integrator, feedback divider.
2Reference to output: N G/(1+G).Standard feedback algebra. At low frequency G ≫ 1 so this is N; at high frequency it rolls off. Lowpass.
3VCO noise to output: 1/(1+G).The VCO's noise enters after the phase detector. At low frequency the loop suppresses it; above the bandwidth it passes. Highpass.
4So the loop bandwidth is a crossover, not a quality setting.Wide bandwidth tracks the reference and suppresses VCO noise; narrow bandwidth rejects reference noise and lets the VCO run free. The optimum is where the two noise densities cross, and that point is measured, not chosen by taste.
5Total output jitter is the integral of the combined phase-noise spectrum over the band of interest.Which is why a jitter specification without an integration band is incomplete — the same part can be quoted at 200 fs or 2 ps depending on limits.

A PLL specification needs five numbers and is usually given two. Output frequency and jitter are not enough. The five are: output frequency range; integrated jitter with its integration band; spurious tone level, since a single reference spur can violate a mask that broadband jitter passes; lock time, which sets how long a power-gated block takes to become usable; and the input reference's own phase noise, since the PLL multiplies it by 20log10N. A block that is specified without the fifth will work on the vendor's evaluation board with a laboratory clock source and fail in the customer's system with a crystal oscillator.

Problem. A clock-and-data-recovery loop and a frequency-synthesis PLL are both PLLs. Why is the CDR's bandwidth chosen by a standard rather than by the designer?
Solution. Because in a CDR the loop bandwidth determines jitter tolerance, which is an interoperability property rather than a performance one. Jitter below the loop bandwidth is tracked by the receiver and causes no error; jitter above it is not tracked and eats the eye directly. So the receiver's bandwidth and the transmitter's permitted jitter spectrum are two halves of one specification: if each vendor chose its own bandwidth, a compliant transmitter and a compliant receiver could still fail together. Standards therefore specify a jitter tolerance mask — how much sinusoidal jitter the receiver must survive at each frequency — which constrains the loop bandwidth from below, and a jitter transfer limit, which constrains it from above so that jitter is not amplified around the loop's peaking and accumulated through a chain of repeaters. The designer's freedom is what remains between the two masks, and in modern standards that gap is narrow.
Problem. Why does a fractional-N synthesiser need a delta-sigma modulator, and what does it cost?
Solution. A divider divides by an integer. To reach a fractional average the divide ratio must be switched between neighbouring integers, and the pattern of switching determines where the resulting phase error lands in frequency. A simple periodic pattern puts it at a low frequency inside the loop bandwidth, where it appears as a fractional spur — a discrete tone that violates masks. A delta-sigma modulator chooses the pattern so that the quantisation error is shaped to high frequency, where the loop filters it. The costs are real: the shaped noise is larger in total than the unshaped noise, so the loop bandwidth must be kept below the point where the shaping rises; a higher-order modulator shapes harder but can produce idle tones if its input sits near a simple fraction, which is why implementations dither the input; and the divider must now handle a range of ratios, which complicates its design. This is a clean example of a digital block bought to relax an analogue constraint, which is the dominant trend in mixed-signal design and the reason the boundary keeps moving toward the digital side.
