X15. Hardware Security, Worked

Security is where a plausible-looking design is most often wrong, because the adversary is not a random input but a person who reads your datasheet. This part covers the parts of hardware security that are arithmetic — how many traces an attack needs, how much entropy a source really has, how long a key survives — because those are the parts a designer can actually bound.

A masked implementation that is not verified at the gate level is usually not masked. Masking is a property of the computation, and synthesis optimises computations. If the tool finds that two shares are combined in a common subexpression — which is exactly the kind of optimisation it is good at — the shares meet in one gate and the first-order leakage returns, while the RTL still looks masked. There is a second, subtler failure: even without optimisation, a glitch in combinational logic can momentarily present a function of both shares, which is why threshold implementations are designed to be glitch-resistant by construction rather than merely share-correct. The verification here is formal leakage analysis on the netlist, not inspection of the source.

X15.2 Entropy: counting what a random number generator really has

Worked example 8. A ring-oscillator TRNG, entropy per bit
Given. Two free-running ring oscillators, one sampled by the other. Jitter accumulates over the sampling interval; the accumulated timing uncertainty is σ = 12 ps and the sampled oscillator's period is T = 400 ps.
Method. The min-entropy of the sampled bit depends on how much of a period the uncertainty spans. A standard stochastic-model estimate is H ≈ 1 − (4/π2)e−4π2σ2/T2, which goes to 1 when the jitter covers a period and to 0 when it is small.

How this is got wrong. By testing the output of the conditioner instead of the source. A cryptographic hash applied to a biased source produces output that passes every statistical test — that is what a hash does — while containing only as much entropy as went in. The test must be applied to the raw source, with an online health test that keeps checking it in the field, because the quantity being relied on (σ) depends on supply noise, temperature and ageing, and an attacker with access to the supply can reduce it deliberately. The second row from the bottom is that attack expressed as arithmetic.
Why the standards insist on min-entropy rather than Shannon entropy. Shannon entropy is an average; an adversary guesses the most likely value. A source that emits 0 with probability 0.9 has Shannon entropy 0.47 bits and min-entropy −log2(0.9) = 0.15 bits, and the second number is the one that bounds an attacker's success. For a key of k bits assembled from such a source, the attacker's work is 2 raised to the total min-entropy, not the total Shannon entropy — a factor of 20.32 per bit, which over 256 bits is a factor of 1024. Using the wrong entropy measure is not conservative rounding, it is a wrong security claim.

The classic differential fault attack on RSA-CRT recovers the private key from one faulty signature, which is why the inverse check — verify the signature before releasing it — is standard there despite its cost. When one fault is enough, probabilistic countermeasures are not.
Problem. A customer asks for a ‘secure’ AES block. What do you ask them before quoting?
Solution. Four questions, because ‘secure’ alone does not specify anything. What is the attacker's physical access? A block in a server that an attacker cannot touch needs no side-channel countermeasures; a smart card that the attacker owns needs every one of them, and the area difference is a factor of three. How many operations per key? This bounds the traces available and therefore the required leakage reduction directly, via the table in X15.1. What certification is required? Common Criteria or FIPS levels prescribe specific countermeasures and specific evidence, and the evidence costs more than the design. What is the throughput and latency requirement? Masking costs randomness per cycle, and a design that must run at line rate may not be able to afford the randomness generation. A quote given without these answers will be wrong by a large factor in one direction or the other, and a vendor who asks them is demonstrating exactly the competence being bought.
Problem. Why is a hardware root of trust harder than storing a key in fuses?
Solution. Because the key is the easy part. A root of trust must establish, at every boot, that the first code to run is the code the owner intended — which requires immutable boot code, a verified signature chain, and protection of the verification itself from the fault and side-channel attacks above. It must survive the states nobody designs for: reset during an update, a power glitch mid-verification, a debug interface left enabled. It needs a way to revoke a compromised key and to roll forward a version counter that cannot be rolled back, which means monotonic storage, which means more fuses and a policy for them. And it must do all this while remaining debuggable in the field by the legitimate owner, which is the requirement that quietly reopens every door the rest closed. The key in fuses is perhaps five per cent of the work; the rest is the state machine around it, and that state machine is where the published attacks land.
