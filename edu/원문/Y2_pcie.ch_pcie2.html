Y2. PCI Express, Generations 3 to 6, Worked

PCIe is the interface a design house is most likely to be asked for and least likely to build from scratch, because the controller is enormous and the compliance burden is severe. It is nevertheless worth understanding in detail, because almost every block a design house sells will sit behind one, and the properties of that link — its latency, its credit behaviour, its error model — propagate into the specification of whatever is attached.

Worked example 61. Why Gen6 went to PAM4 and what it cost
Given. Gen5 runs 32 GT/s NRZ; Gen6 runs 64 GT/s. The channel budget is roughly unchanged, because the connectors and boards are the same.
Method. Compare the Nyquist frequency each modulation requires and the eye each produces, then note what follows.
Gen5 NRZ Nyquist16 GHz
Gen6 at 64 GT/s with NRZ would need32 GHz
Gen6 PAM4 symbol rate32 GBd
Gen6 PAM4 Nyquist16 GHz
PAM4 levels4
Eye height relative to NRZ at equal amplitude0.333
SNR penalty9.542 dB
Consequencea raw BER near 10−6, so FEC becomes mandatory
How this is got wrong. By reading PAM4 as free bandwidth. It keeps the Nyquist frequency — and therefore the channel loss — unchanged, which is the whole point, and it pays 9.5 dB of eye for it. That payment forces three things into the specification at once: forward error correction, which adds latency; a flit-based protocol, because FEC needs fixed-size blocks and the old variable-length TLP framing does not provide them; and retry at the flit level rather than the packet level. Gen6 is therefore not an incremental speed bump but a protocol change, and any IP behind it sees different latency statistics.

‘Trains at Gen1, fails at Gen3’ is a diagnosis, not a symptom, and it is worth knowing why. Every PCIe link starts at 2.5 GT/s with no equalisation, because both ends must be able to talk before they can negotiate. The move to Gen3 and beyond runs a four-phase equalisation procedure in Recovery in which each end requests transmitter coefficient settings from the other and evaluates the result. A link that trains at Gen1 and fails above it has working wires and a failing equalisation: the channel is worse than the preset assumes, the far end's coefficient requests are being ignored or mishandled, or the receiver's evaluation is reporting success when the eye is marginal. The debug sequence is therefore: read the link status registers for the actual speed and width; read the lane error status; capture the equalisation phase the link fails in; and only then look at the board. Controllers that expose per-phase equalisation status are much faster to bring up, and this is a feature worth asking a vendor for.

Worked example 62. Why a DMA engine behind PCIe needs deep outstanding-read support
Given. A Gen4 x8 link, 4 µs round-trip read latency to host memory, requesting 512-byte reads. The engine must sustain 12 GB/s.
Method. Bandwidth–delay product again, exactly as in Part X11 — the formula does not care that the wire is now a PCIe link.
Gen4 x8 usable bandwidth15.75 GB/s
Required bandwidth12 GB/s
Bytes in flight needed46.88 kB
512-byte reads outstanding94
Tag space needed (10-bit tags allow 768)94 of 768
Completion buffer required46.88 kB
How this is got wrong. By sizing from the link rate and ignoring the latency. A device with 32 outstanding reads on this link achieves 512 × 32 / 4 µs = 4 GB/s regardless of the link being capable of 15. The second trap is the completion buffer: those bytes in flight must land somewhere, so the buffer is not optional and it is tens of kilobytes. Outstanding-transaction capability and completion-buffer size are the two numbers that decide whether a PCIe device performs, and they belong on the first page of its datasheet, not in an appendix.

Y2.4 What the corpus contains, and what it does not

This book's code appendix includes 198,332 lines of a working open-source PCIe datapath: DMA engines, TLP generation and parsing, and AXI bridging. It is genuinely useful and it is worth being precise about what it is. It is the transaction and data-link layer above a vendor hard macro — the PHY, the LTSSM, the equalisation procedure and the serialiser are not in it and cannot be, because they are analogue and foundry-specific.

Compliance is the commercial fact about PCIe. A controller that works is not a controller that can be sold: the PCI-SIG compliance programme, its test fixtures, its interoperability workshops and the membership that permits them are a substantial and recurring cost, and a customer buying a PCIe controller is buying that compliance as much as the RTL. For a one-person design house the rational position is above the controller, not inside it — build the accelerator, the DMA engine, the protocol offload that sits behind a licensed controller, and let the customer bring the controller they have already qualified.
Problem. A customer asks you to quote for ‘a PCIe Gen4 endpoint with a custom accelerator’. How do you structure the quote?
Solution. Split it explicitly, and say why in the quote itself. Item one: the accelerator — your design, your verification, your price. Item two: integration with a controller the customer licenses, quoted as integration effort with the controller named, because the effort depends heavily on which one; a controller with a clean AXI-Stream application interface is a fortnight and one with an idiosyncratic interface is two months. Item three: what you are not supplying — the controller licence, the PHY, the compliance testing, and the driver, each listed with who you expect to provide it. A quote that silently includes the controller either loses on price to competitors who excluded it, or wins and then discovers the licence costs more than the project. Being explicit about scope is not a weakness in a proposal; it is the clearest signal that the quoter has done this before, and experienced buyers read it that way.
Problem. Gen6 introduced FEC. What does that do to the latency your block sees, and why can the FEC not simply be made stronger to allow a cheaper channel?
Solution. FEC operates on fixed-size flits, so the receiver must collect a whole flit before it can correct it, and the correction itself takes time. The added latency is small in absolute terms — tens of nanoseconds — but it is added to every transaction, and for a latency-sensitive accelerator performing fine-grained host synchronisation it can matter more than the doubled bandwidth helps. It also changes the distribution: retries now happen at flit granularity with a different tail than Gen5's packet-level replay. Stronger FEC is not the answer to a worse channel because latency is the constraint that a stronger code violates first: a longer code corrects more and waits longer, and PCIe's entire value proposition rests on being a low-latency load/store fabric rather than a network. The design point was chosen to bound added latency, and the code strength followed from that bound — not the other way round, which is the usual direction in communications and the reason PCIe's FEC looks weak to someone arriving from Ethernet.
