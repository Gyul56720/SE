Y5. The Commercial Mechanics of Selling an IP Block

A design house that cannot price, license and support its work does not have a business, it has a hobby with good documentation. This part gives the arithmetic of the commercial side with the same seriousness as the technical parts, because the failure modes are just as specific and rather less discussed.

Worked example 65. When a royalty beats a buyout, from the customer's side
Given. A block licensed at $150 000 plus $0.20 per unit, or $600 000 paid up. The customer's forecast is 1 million units over three years, but forecasts in this market are routinely out by 3× in both directions.
Method. Compute the break-even volume, then compute what each party's exposure is above and below it. The break-even is arithmetic; the interesting part is the asymmetry around it.
Break-even volume2,250,000
Royalty model cost at 1M units3.5000×105 USD
Buyout cost600,000 USD
Royalty cost at 0.3M units (forecast ÷ 3)2.1000×105 USD
Royalty cost at 3M units7.5000×105 USD
Your revenue at 3M units under buyout600,000 USD
Your revenue at 3M units under royalty7.5000×105 USD
How this is got wrong. By negotiating the break-even and not the distribution. Both parties can compute 2.25 million units; what decides the deal is who is more confident and who can bear the downside. A one-person house with no cash reserve should prefer the larger up-front fee even at a worse expected value, because surviving to the next contract dominates expected value when a single bad quarter ends the business. The standard compromise is a royalty with a cap and a floor, which bounds both parties' exposure and is easier to agree than either extreme.

The known-issue list is the item that distinguishes a professional supplier, and it is counter-intuitive to first-time vendors. Every block has limitations: a configuration that was never verified, a corner that fails a lint rule for a defensible reason, a performance figure that holds only above a certain clock ratio. A customer who finds these themselves concludes you did not know; a customer who reads them in your release notes concludes you did. The second customer calls you when they have a problem instead of calling your competitor. Write the list, keep it honest, and put dates and workarounds beside each entry.

Queries per customer fall with experience because the documentation improves and the frequent questions get answered in it. At twenty customers support is a full-time job, which for a one-person house means either hiring, raising prices to slow growth, or investing hard in documentation and self-service — and the third is the only one available immediately.
Worked example 66. Pricing that accounts for support
Given. You expect 8 customers over three years for a block that took 44 weeks to build. Target: recover the build and support and earn a living. Your cost of living plus overhead is $120 000 a year.
Method. Total the engineering, add the support from the table above, and divide. Then compare with what the market will bear, because the two are independent numbers and the smaller one wins.

How this is got wrong. By pricing from cost and ignoring the market. The last row is the real risk: with three customers instead of eight, the price that covers cost is more than double, and there is no reason a customer will pay it. The standard mitigations are to start with services revenue, which is paid whether or not the block sells; to sell the first block below cost to a reference customer in exchange for a public reference, which is worth more than the margin; and to build blocks whose adjacent variants share most of the effort, so the second customer in a family costs a fraction of the first.

Provenance of the source you ship is a hard requirement, not a formality. If any part of your block derives from code with a copyleft licence, or from a previous employer's work, or from a reference design with redistribution limits, a customer's legal review will find it — they scan for this — and the deal ends there. Keep a per-file record of origin, do not paste from sources you cannot name, and be able to produce that record on request. The code appendix of this book is an illustration of the standard: every file reproduced in it is named with its repository and its line count, and a reader can trace any line back to its source.
Problem. A prospective customer asks for a three-month free evaluation with full source. How do you respond?
Solution. Not with a flat refusal, and not with the source. The request is reasonable in intent — they cannot buy what they cannot assess — and unreasonable in form, because full source for three months is the product. The standard structures, in increasing order of what you give away: an evaluation package of documentation, the datasheet, the register map and simulation results, which costs you nothing and answers most questions; an encrypted or obfuscated netlist or FPGA bitstream they can integrate and measure but not read, which answers the performance question; and a paid evaluation licence whose fee is credited against the full licence if they proceed, which aligns both parties and filters out tyre-kickers at no cost to a serious buyer. The paid evaluation is the one to propose; a customer who will not pay a small fee to evaluate is very unlikely to pay a large one to license.
Problem. Your block has been shipping for a year when a customer reports a bug that affects every unit they have built. What do you do, in order?
Solution. Reproduce it before saying anything about cause. Ask for the configuration, the stimulus and the failing condition, and reproduce it in your own environment; a report is a hypothesis. Then determine the blast radius — which configurations, which versions, which other customers — because the answer determines whether this is one email or a notification to everyone. Then notify affected customers before they find it, with what is known, what is not, and when the next update will come; a vendor who reports their own bug keeps the account, and one who is caught concealing it does not. Then fix, with a regression test that would have caught it, and say in the release notes what that test is. Finally, ask why the verification plan missed it and fix the plan, not just the code — Part X8's mutation argument applies directly: if the suite did not catch this, inject it and find out what else of the same shape it would miss.
