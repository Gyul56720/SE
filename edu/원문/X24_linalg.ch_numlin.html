X24. Numerical Linear Algebra in Finite Precision

Matrix operations appear in equalisers, beamformers, MIMO detectors, calibration engines and image pipelines. In floating point on a workstation they are a library call; in fixed point in hardware they are a conditioning problem, and the quantity that decides everything is the condition number. This part makes that quantity concrete.

X24.1 Conditioning: how many bits the problem destroys

The condition number, and what it costs you
StepJustification
1For Ax = b, perturb b by δb. Then δx = A−1δb.Linearity.
2Bounding the relative error gives ‖δx‖/‖x‖ ≤ κ(A) ‖δb‖/‖b‖, with κ = ‖A‖ ‖A−1‖.Combine the two norm inequalities. In the 2-norm, κ is the ratio of largest to smallest singular value.
3So log2κ bits of the answer are destroyed by the problem itself, before any algorithm is chosen.A condition number of 106 costs about 20 bits. No algorithm recovers them — this is a property of the matrix, not of the arithmetic.
4A backward stable algorithm adds only about log2(n) bits of its own.It returns the exact answer to a slightly perturbed problem. Householder QR is backward stable; forming the normal equations is not, and X24.2 measures the difference.
5Word length needed ≈ bits wanted + log2κ + a few.The practical sizing rule for any matrix operation in hardware.

Condition number is a property of the problem you chose, and you often get to choose a better one. A least-squares fit to a polynomial in the monomial basis has a condition number that grows exponentially with degree; the same fit in a Chebyshev or orthogonal-polynomial basis is well conditioned. A channel matrix that is ill conditioned can be regularised — add λI — trading a small bias for an enormous variance reduction. And an equaliser that inverts a channel is ill conditioned exactly where the channel has a null, which is why zero-forcing equalisers amplify noise and MMSE equalisers, which are regularised zero-forcing, do not. Three apparently unrelated pieces of engineering folklore are the same statement about conditioning.

How this is got wrong. By choosing the normal equations because the operation count is half. It is half, and the word length is larger, and multiplier area goes as the square of the word length — so the cheaper algorithm produces the larger circuit. Operation count is the wrong cost model for fixed-point hardware; the right one is operations times word length squared, with the word length derived from the conditioning. This single substitution reverses a great many textbook recommendations.
Why Givens rotations and CORDIC are made for each other. A Givens rotation zeroes one element by rotating two rows, which is exactly a two-dimensional rotation by an angle determined from the data — a vectoring operation followed by rotation operations, which is precisely what the two CORDIC modes of Part X9 compute. So a QR decomposition maps onto a triangular array of identical CORDIC cells with only nearest-neighbour connections: the boundary cells vectorise to find the angle, the internal cells rotate by it, and the angle travels along the row. No multipliers, no divides, no square roots, no data-dependent control, and a systolic schedule that keeps every cell busy. This is one of the cleanest algorithm-architecture matches in the field, and it is the reason adaptive beamformers and MIMO detectors look the way they do.

Iterative refinement is the trick that makes low precision usable, and it is worth stating exactly. Solve Ax = b in low precision to get x0; compute the residual r = b − Ax0 in high precision; solve Aδ = r in low precision again; add. Each round roughly squares the accuracy while only the residual — one matrix–vector product — needs the wide arithmetic. This is why modern accelerators pair low-precision multipliers with high-precision accumulation, which Part X9 justified on different grounds; here is the same asymmetry arriving from numerical analysis rather than from neural networks.

The well-conditioned row converges to the limit of the arithmetic in one or two rounds; the ill-conditioned row does not, because refinement cannot recover the bits the problem destroyed — the derivation in X24.1 said so, and this measures it.
Problem. You must invert a 4×4 covariance matrix every microsecond in hardware. What structure, and what word length?
Solution. Structure first: do not invert it. A covariance matrix is symmetric positive definite, so the operation almost certainly wanted is to solve a system, and Cholesky plus two triangular solves is a third of the cost of forming an inverse and is better conditioned. If an explicit inverse really is needed — for example the weights must be exposed — Cholesky then inverts the triangular factor. Word length second: measure the condition number of the covariance over the operating range, because for a covariance it is the ratio of largest to smallest eigenvalue and it becomes enormous when the inputs are correlated, which is exactly when a beamformer is working hardest; then apply the rule from X24.1, bits wanted plus log2κ plus a margin. Third, regularise: adding a small λI — diagonal loading, in the beamforming literature — bounds κ by construction, costs a tiny bias, and converts an unbounded word-length requirement into a fixed one. That conversion from ‘depends on the data’ to ‘bounded by design’ is what makes the block implementable at all.
