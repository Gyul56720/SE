X31. Analogue Building Blocks, Budgeted

A digital designer does not size transistors, but does have to say whether an analogue requirement is reasonable before committing to an architecture. The quantities that answer that are few and computable: thermal noise, matching, gain-bandwidth and settling. This part gives them.

X31.1 kT/C — the noise floor you cannot design away

Why sampled noise depends only on the capacitor
StepJustification
1A switch has resistance R; its thermal noise density is 4kTR V²/Hz.Johnson–Nyquist.
2The RC network's noise bandwidth is 1/(4RC).Integrating a single-pole response gives π/2 times the 3 dB bandwidth.
3So the sampled noise power is 4kTR · 1/(4RC) = kT/C.The resistance cancels. A better switch does not help; only a bigger capacitor does.
4Doubling resolution means halving the noise voltage, which means quadrupling the capacitor.And capacitance is area, and it is also the load the driver must charge, so power rises too.
5Each extra bit of a sampled system costs 4× the capacitor and roughly 4× the power.This single relation explains the shape of every converter's energy-per-conversion curve above about 10 bits.

Worked example 30. Can this ADC specification be met?
Given. A customer asks for a 16-bit, 100 MS/s SAR ADC on 1 V supply, ‘small area’.
Method. Work the kT/C requirement, then the settling requirement, then compare against what the process offers.

How this is got wrong. By treating ‘16-bit’ and ‘100 MS/s’ as independent requirements. The noise requirement forces a large capacitor; the speed requirement forces that capacitor to settle in picoseconds, which forces a very low switch resistance and therefore an enormous switch, which loads the previous stage. This is why 16 bits at 100 MS/s is a time-interleaved or pipelined architecture and not a single SAR, and why the answer to the customer is a question about their actual SNR requirement in the signal band — if they need 16 bits over 1 MHz rather than over 50 MHz, oversampling changes the arithmetic completely.

X31.2 Matching: why analogue area is set by statistics

Pelgrom's law
StepJustification
1Threshold voltage varies between nominally identical devices because of random dopant fluctuation and line-edge roughness.A counting phenomenon: fewer dopants under a smaller gate means larger relative variation.
2The standard deviation of the mismatch between two devices is σΔVth = AVT/√(WL).Pelgrom's law, verified across many processes. AVT is a process constant of a few mV·µm.
3So halving the mismatch requires four times the area.The same 4× law as kT/C, arriving from a completely different mechanism.
4A comparator's input-referred offset is this mismatch; a current mirror's error is proportional to it.Which sets the minimum size of every matched pair in a converter.
5Digital calibration converts area into computation.Measure the offset once, store it, subtract it. This is the dominant trend in mixed-signal design and the reason the digital content of converters keeps growing.

Without calibration, matching alone limits an uncalibrated comparator to about eight bits at sane areas. Every converter above that resolution calibrates, and the calibration engine is digital — which is how a digital designer ends up owning a large part of an analogue block.
The calibration trade, stated as arithmetic. Suppose a comparator needs 1 mV of offset. Pelgrom says that costs about 12 µm² of gate area per device, and in a 64-way time-interleaved ADC that is repeated 64 times, in the fastest and most power-hungry part of the chip. Alternatively, build a minimum-size comparator with 10 mV of offset and add a trim DAC plus a digital loop that measures and cancels it. The trim DAC is small, the loop is a counter and a comparator, and the whole thing is synthesised. The crossover is far to the digital side and moves further every process node, because digital area shrinks and AVT improves only slowly. This is the single clearest instance of the general trend this book keeps meeting: buy analogue precision with digital computation.

Problem. Your digital block shares a supply with an ADC and the customer reports spurs at your clock frequency. Whose problem is it?
Solution. Both of yours, and the useful response is to find out which mechanism rather than which party. There are three paths and they are distinguishable by measurement. Supply coupling: your switching current modulates the shared rail, and the converter's finite power-supply rejection turns that into an input offset; test by changing your block's activity and watching whether the spur amplitude tracks, and by separating the supplies if the board permits. Substrate coupling: switching current injects into the substrate and reaches the converter's sensitive nodes; this is layout, and the remedies are guard rings, deep n-well isolation and distance. Clock coupling: your clock capacitively couples into the sampling network, which is the worst of the three because it lands exactly at a fixed frequency and its harmonics. The digital side's contribution, and it is a real one, is to reduce di/dt: spread the clock tree's switching with deliberate skew, gate clocks aggressively so the average current is lower, add on-chip decoupling, and avoid large simultaneous switching events. None of that is exotic, and all of it is far cheaper than asking the analogue designer for another 20 dB of supply rejection, which may not be available at any price.
