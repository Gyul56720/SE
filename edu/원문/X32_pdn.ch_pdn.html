X32. The Power Delivery Network, Worked

Every timing number in Part X2 assumed a supply voltage. The network that delivers it is a distributed RLC structure whose impedance must be low across six decades of frequency, and when it is not, the result is a voltage droop that shows up as a timing failure nobody can reproduce. This is a digital designer's problem even though it looks like an analogue one.

From a droop budget to an impedance specification

Worked example 31. Sizing on-die decoupling capacitance
Given. A block draws 5 A when active and is clock-gated. On wake-up the current rises in 2 ns. The package inductance is 100 pH and the droop budget is 40 mV.
Method. The package cannot supply current faster than its inductance allows, so on-die capacitance must cover the interval until the package catches up. Compute the charge needed and hence the capacitance.

How this is got wrong. By budgeting only the resistive drop. At a nanosecond timescale the package is an inductor, not a wire, and Ldi/dt dominates the static IR term by a large factor. The remedy is not lower resistance but local charge storage, and the area it costs is real — on-die decoupling is commonly several per cent of die area and in some designs more than ten. A second, cheaper remedy belongs to the digital designer: ramp the current instead of stepping it, by staging the clock-gate release across several cycles, which is a few gates of logic and can reduce the required capacitance by a large factor.

Every inductance in the hierarchy resonates with the capacitance on the load side of it, and at resonance the impedance is Q times the series resistance. A high-Q peak is the dangerous case: if the current spectrum has energy there, the droop is far worse than the target impedance suggests.
The first-droop, second-droop, third-droop language engineers use is just this resonance hierarchy, and the dangerous one is usually the mid-frequency droop. The first droop (hundreds of megahertz) is handled by on-die capacitance; the third (kilohertz) by the regulator's control loop. The second — tens to a couple of hundred megahertz, set by package inductance against on-die capacitance — sits in a gap between the two remedies and is where the highest-Q peak usually lands. It is also, unhelpfully, right where a processor's activity changes: a loop that alternates between a heavy and a light phase at that rate excites it directly, which is the origin of the classic ‘this benchmark fails and that one does not’ report. The digital remedies are to add damping (series resistance in some decoupling, deliberately), to spread activity in time, and to use an adaptive clock that stretches the period when a droop is detected — which converts a functional failure into a small performance loss and is now common in large processors.

Problem. Your block passes timing in every corner and fails intermittently in silicon at high activity. How do you decide whether it is the PDN?
Solution. Look for the signature, which is specific and distinguishable. A PDN-induced failure is activity dependent rather than pattern dependent: it appears when many things switch at once, not when a particular logical case occurs, so a test that toggles a lot fails while a functionally harder but quieter test passes. It moves with supply and with frequency in a particular way: raising the supply helps more than it should for a simple timing problem, because it increases the margin against droop as well as speeding the logic. It correlates with di/dt, not with average power, so a test with the same average current but smoother activity passes. The measurements that settle it are an on-die droop monitor if one exists — and if none exists, that is the lesson for the next design, because a ring-oscillator-based droop detector is a few hundred gates — and an experiment that staggers the wake-up of the block's clock-gating domains, which changes only di/dt. If staggering fixes it, the PDN is the cause and the cheapest fix is the staggering you just used to diagnose it.
