X36. Cryptographic Engines, Sized

Crypto IP is one of the most commonly licensed categories, and it is unusual in that its correctness is binary, its specifications are published and testable, and its cost is dominated by a small number of arithmetic primitives. That combination makes it a good first product for a design house and a good subject for exact sizing.

Post-quantum cryptography is an opportunity for design houses precisely because it is new. The standards are recent, the hardware literature is thin, the incumbent crypto-IP vendors' portfolios were built for RSA and ECC, and the core operation — the number-theoretic transform — is an FFT over a finite field, which means Part X6's entire architectural analysis transfers: the same radix choices, the same twiddle-ROM symmetries, the same memory-versus-multiplier trade. A team that has built an FFT has most of what an NTT needs, with the arithmetic replaced by modular arithmetic, and that is an unusually direct path from an existing competence to a new market.

X36.2 AES: where the area actually goes

The S-box is the block
StepJustification
1AES-128 is ten rounds of SubBytes, ShiftRows, MixColumns, AddRoundKey.Specification.
2ShiftRows is wiring; AddRoundKey is 128 XORs; MixColumns is a fixed GF(28) matrix — shifts and XORs.All three are nearly free.
3SubBytes applies a 256-entry nonlinear substitution to each of 16 bytes.This is the whole cost.
4As a table it is 16 × 2048 bits of ROM or 16 × ~700 gates synthesised.The naive implementation.
5Or compute it: the S-box is inversion in GF(28) plus an affine map, and inversion decomposes through GF((24)2).Canright's tower-field construction reduces the S-box to roughly a third of the table's area, at the cost of more logic depth. 6A round can use 16 S-boxes (one cycle per round), 4, or 1.Throughput scales linearly with S-box count and so does area, which makes AES one of the cleanest area/throughput families to parameterise — and therefore a good product, because one design serves many customers.

How this is got wrong. By sizing AES and forgetting the mode. Counter mode parallelises perfectly — each block's keystream is independent — so AES scales by replication. The GCM authentication tag is a sequential multiply-accumulate in GF(2128), which does not parallelise naively; making it keep up requires the standard trick of precomputing powers of the hash key and processing several blocks at once, which costs several 128-bit carry-less multipliers and their key storage. At high line rates the authenticator is larger than the cipher, and a datasheet that quotes only AES throughput has quoted the easy half.

X36.3 Public key: modular multiplication is the whole problem

Montgomery multiplication, and why division disappears
StepJustification
1Modular multiplication needs ab mod n; the reduction is a division, which is expensive (Part X9).And it is needed thousands of times per operation.
2Montgomery's idea: work with a̅ = aR mod n for R = 2k > n.A change of representation.
3Then REDC(x) = (x + (xn′ mod R)n)/R reduces using only multiplications and shifts, because dividing by R is a shift.The division is gone. n′ is precomputed once.
4Conversion in and out costs two extra multiplications, amortised over the whole exponentiation.Negligible over thousands of operations.
5So an RSA engine is: a wide multiplier, an adder, a shifter, and a controller.And the wide multiplier is 80–90 % of the area, which is why the literature is about multiplier architectures and not about protocols.

The multiplication counts are the standard estimates: square-and-multiply for RSA with the Chinese remainder theorem halving the modulus, and roughly a dozen field operations per elliptic-curve point addition. The table explains the industry's migration: ECC at 256 bits gives comparable security to RSA at 3072 and costs orders of magnitude less work.
A public-key engine that is merely correct is dangerous. The classical attacks are not on the mathematics but on the implementation: a square-and-multiply loop that branches on key bits leaks the key through timing and through power (Part X15), and a Montgomery ladder — which performs the same operations regardless of the bit — costs perhaps 30 % more work and removes the leak. Likewise a conditional subtraction at the end of a reduction is a data-dependent branch, so implementations make it unconditional. Constant-time behaviour must be a stated requirement and a verified property, not a side effect, and it is one of the few properties where a formal argument (no branch or memory access depends on secret data) is both achievable and genuinely convincing.
Problem. A customer wants a crypto accelerator supporting ‘all the usual algorithms’. How do you scope it?
Solution. Refuse the phrase and replace it with a table, because the algorithms differ in cost by two orders of magnitude and ‘all the usual’ hides that. Ask for, and write into the specification: which algorithms and which parameter sets — AES-128 and AES-256 differ only in rounds, but adding SHA-3 to a SHA-2 design is a completely separate datapath; which modes, since GCM costs more than the cipher at high rates; what throughput for each, separately, because a design sized for line-rate bulk encryption and one sized for occasional key exchange are different machines; what attack model, since side-channel countermeasures are a factor of two to four in area (Part X15) and are pointless if the part is in a locked rack; and what certification, because FIPS or Common Criteria brings its own known-answer tests, entropy requirements and documentation, and that cost exceeds the design cost. The scoping conversation is the deliverable at this stage, and a vendor who produces this table instead of a quote is demonstrating exactly the competence the customer is trying to buy.
