Z3. The Quality Bar: What ‘Sellable’ Means as Numbers

‘Industry level’ is not a feeling. A customer's technical evaluation is a list of questions with numerical answers, and a block either has them or does not. This part is that list, with the acceptance criterion for each and the command that produces it. A block that passes every row is sellable; one that passes most of them is a prototype.

Nine of the thirteen are runnable by the agent, unattended. The four that are not — judging escapes, CDC analysis, documentation, and deciding what the block should do — are the ones this volume says belong to you.
The bar is not ‘no known bugs’; it is ‘here is the evidence, and here is what is not covered’. Every real IP block ships with limitations, and Part Y5 argues that naming them is what distinguishes a professional supplier. The gate list above is what converts that from a posture into a document: each row either passes, or fails with a stated reason and a workaround. A customer can evaluate that. They cannot evaluate an assurance.

Z3.2 Running the whole bar in one command

A gate that requires remembering to run it is a gate that will be skipped in the week it matters. The repository's own discipline applies: one command, from a clean checkout, that runs everything and refuses to report green on anything it could not check.

edu/house/release.sh — the release gate

Worked example 91. What the gate costs to run
Given. Five blocks, the regression at 20 seeds each, a mutation pass, lint and synthesis.
Method. Estimate from the measured per-step times in this book's own runs.
Regression, 600 stimuli0.03 s per seed per block
20 seeds × 5 blocks3 s
Self-check0.15 s
Mutation pass, 5 blocks≈60 s
Lintseconds
Synthesisseconds to minutes per block
Whole gatea few minutes
How often it should runevery push, and nightly
How this is got wrong. By assuming the gate is expensive and running it at release. At a few minutes it is cheap enough to run on every push, and a gate that runs on every push finds the regression on the commit that caused it rather than three weeks later. The expensive parts — a full mutation pass over a large block, a long synthesis — belong in the nightly run, which costs nothing at all because nobody is waiting.

Two columns because they serve different purposes. The internal column is what you refuse to go home below; the release column is what a customer's evaluation will find. Setting the internal bar lower than the release bar is how a release becomes a scramble.
Problem. A customer's technical evaluation asks for your verification plan. What do you send?
Solution. Not a narrative. Send four things, and their existence is most of the answer. The gate list of Z3.1 with each row's current value — this is a table of facts and it is what they are actually trying to establish. The mutation report, escapes included, because it is the one artefact that demonstrates the suite bites rather than merely runs, and very few suppliers have one; including the escapes with their judgements is more convincing than a higher score without them. The known-issue list, dated, with workarounds. And the command they can run themselves on the delivered package to reproduce every number. The fourth is the one that converts the first three from claims into evidence, and it is the reason the release gate must run from a clean checkout rather than from your working directory — if it only works on your machine, they will find that out, and it will cost more than the bug it hides.
