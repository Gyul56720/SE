X39. Clock Generation, Distribution and Time Synchronisation

Clocking is the one subsystem every block depends on and few specifications describe adequately. This part covers the three parts an IP designer must be able to reason about: how the clock is made, how it is delivered, and how time is agreed between chips.

X39.1 Spread spectrum: trading a specification for a specification

Why modulating the clock reduces measured emissions
StepJustification
1Radiated emission is measured in a receiver with a defined resolution bandwidth — 120 kHz for CISPR above 30 MHz.The regulation specifies the measurement, not the physics.
2An unmodulated clock puts all its harmonic energy in one line, far narrower than that bandwidth.So the receiver sees the whole harmonic.
3Modulating the frequency by ±0.5 % spreads each harmonic over ±0.5 % of its frequency.The nth harmonic spreads n times as far, so higher harmonics benefit more.
4The receiver now sees only the fraction of the energy inside its bandwidth, so the reading falls by roughly 10 log10(spread/RBW) dB.The total radiated energy is unchanged. The compliance number improves; the physics does not.
5Cost: the clock period now varies, so every synchronous budget must hold at the fastest excursion, and any PLL downstream must track the modulation.Which is why a SerDes reference clock's spread must be negotiated — the CDR's loop bandwidth must exceed the modulation rate or it cannot follow.

Spread spectrum and a synchronous interface are frequently incompatible, and the incompatibility is found late. Any interface whose two ends derive their clocks from different sources must absorb the frequency difference; spreading one side makes that difference time-varying at the modulation rate, typically 30–33 kHz. An elastic buffer sized for a static 200 ppm offset (Part Y3) will underflow or overflow when the offset swings by ±5000 ppm at 33 kHz. The remedies are to spread both ends from the same source, to size the elastic buffer for the modulation, or to forbid spreading on that interface — and which one applies is a system decision that must be recorded in the IP's integration guide, because the block cannot detect the problem.

Why a block should provide one clock input and do its own gating, rather than accepting several clocks. Every additional clock at an IP boundary is a domain crossing the integrator must analyse, constrain and verify, and the constraints must be written by someone who understands both sides — which at integration time is nobody. A block that takes one clock and derives its internal enables is far easier to integrate, and the power saving from gating is identical. Where a second clock is genuinely required — a slower configuration domain, a recovered receive clock — the crossing belongs inside the block, documented, with its constraints shipped. That is a concrete piece of value a vendor adds, and its absence is a concrete integration cost.

X39.3 Agreeing on time: IEEE 1588 and the hardware it needs

Why timestamping must be in the PHY
StepJustification
1Two nodes exchange timestamped messages; the offset is ((t2−t1) − (t4−t3))/2.The standard two-way exchange.
2This assumes the path is symmetric.Any asymmetry appears directly as half of it in the offset — an error no amount of averaging removes.
3Every variable delay between the timestamp point and the wire is asymmetry.Software stacks, driver queues, interconnect arbitration, and even FIFO occupancy in the MAC.
4So the timestamp must be taken as close to the wire as possible, in hardware, at a defined reference plane.Typically the start-of-frame delimiter crossing the MII interface, which is why PTP support is a PHY and MAC feature rather than a software one.
5Residual asymmetry (different cable lengths, different PHY latencies for TX and RX) must be calibrated and subtracted.Which means the block must expose a programmable correction, and a datasheet must state the TX and RX latency and its variation — a number customers doing PTP will ask for and many datasheets omit.

Problem. Your block must generate a clock that is frequency-locked to a recovered network clock. What are the options?
Solution. Three, and they differ in what they require from the system rather than in accuracy. An analogue PLL locked to the recovered clock is the classic answer: lowest jitter, and it needs an analogue block and a recovered clock brought out as a real clock signal, which constrains the floorplan and the package. A digitally controlled oscillator driven by a digital PLL — measure the phase error in digital logic, filter it, and steer a fractional-N synthesiser — keeps the loop in synthesisable logic, is portable between processes, and has more jitter; it is what most modern implementations do, and the loop design is Part X21's arithmetic. Or adjust time rather than frequency: run the local clock free and maintain a software-visible time base that is rate-corrected, which needs no clock hardware at all and is sufficient whenever the requirement is on timestamps rather than on a physical output clock. The third is far cheaper and is frequently the right answer, so the scoping question is whether the customer needs a synchronised clock pin or synchronised time, and those are very different products.
