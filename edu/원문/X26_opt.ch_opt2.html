X26. Optimisation, the Tool Behind Every EDA Algorithm

Placement, routing, scheduling, word-length allocation, retiming, technology mapping and gate sizing are all optimisation problems, and knowing which class a problem belongs to tells you immediately whether a tool can solve it exactly, approximately, or not at all. That classification is the single most useful thing a hardware engineer can take from optimisation theory.

The reason gate sizing is solved and placement is not. Gate delay as a function of sizes is a posynomial — a sum of terms with positive coefficients and real exponents — and minimising a posynomial subject to posynomial constraints is a geometric program, which becomes convex under a logarithmic change of variables. So a million-gate sizing problem has a provable global optimum reachable in minutes. Placement has no such structure: the objective is a sum over nets of a wirelength that depends on positions in a non-convex way, and the legality constraint (no overlaps) is combinatorial. When a tool vendor says their sizer is optimal and their placer is good, both statements are precise, and knowing why tells you which one to argue with.

X26.2 Convexity, and how to recognise it in your own problem

What convexity buys, and why
StepJustification
1A set is convex if the segment between any two of its points stays inside.Definition.
2A function is convex if its epigraph is a convex set — equivalently, if the chord lies above the curve.Definition.
3For a convex problem, every local minimum is global.Suppose a local minimum x and a better point y; the segment from x to y is feasible and the function along it lies below the chord, so points arbitrarily close to x beat it, contradicting local minimality.
4So a descent method cannot get stuck, and a duality gap of zero gives a certificate of optimality.The certificate is the practical payoff: you can prove to a customer that no better answer exists, rather than saying the tool stopped.
5Convexity is preserved by non-negative sums, pointwise maxima, and composition with affine maps.These three rules are how you recognise convexity in practice — build the objective from convex pieces and it is convex by construction.

How this is got wrong. By throwing away the relaxation once you have rounded. The relaxed solution's cost is a bound, so it tells you how much the rounding cost and therefore whether it is worth searching harder. A greedy word-length search that reports ‘we found 148 kGE’ says nothing; one that reports ‘148 kGE against a lower bound of 141’ says the search is within five per cent and can stop. Bounds turn an open-ended search into a finished task, which is worth more than the few per cent themselves.

Static timing analysis is longest-path on a directed acyclic graph, and saying so demystifies it. Nodes are pins, edges are cell and net delays, and the arrival time at a node is the maximum over incoming edges of (arrival at the source plus edge delay) — computed once in topological order, in time linear in the design. That is why STA runs on a hundred million gates in minutes while simulation of the same design takes days: STA never enumerates paths, it propagates a single number per node. It also explains the tool's two famous characteristics. Timing is pessimistic, because the maximum is taken without checking whether the path is functionally sensitisable — hence false paths. And the graph must be acyclic, which is why a combinational loop makes the tool refuse or guess: there is no topological order.

Problem. Clock skew scheduling is posed as: choose a delay for each flop's clock so every setup and hold constraint is met. Why is this solvable exactly, when placement is not?
Solution. Because every constraint has the form tj − ti ≤ cij — a difference constraint — where the t are the clock arrival times and the constants come from the setup and hold inequalities of Part X2. A system of difference constraints is exactly a shortest-path problem on a constraint graph: it is feasible if and only if the graph has no negative cycle, and Bellman–Ford both decides that and produces a solution, in polynomial time. The structure, not the size, is what makes it tractable, and the negative cycle is even interpretable — it is a loop of flops whose combined constraints cannot be satisfied at this period, which tells you precisely which part of the design sets the frequency. Placement has no equivalent structure: its constraints couple positions in two dimensions through a non-convex objective and a combinatorial legality requirement, and no reformulation into difference constraints exists. Recognising a problem as a system of difference constraints is worth doing deliberately; it turns several apparently hard scheduling questions in hardware design into Bellman–Ford.
