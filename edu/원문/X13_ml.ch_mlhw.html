X13. Machine-Learning Hardware, Worked

Neural-network accelerators are the largest category of new IP by value, and they are also the category where the gap between a quoted peak number and delivered performance is widest. The arithmetic that closes that gap is the roofline model and its refinements, and it is worth doing carefully because it decides the architecture before a line of RTL is written.

Depthwise convolution is the instructive row. It has excellent parameter efficiency — which is why MobileNet-style networks use it — and terrible arithmetic intensity, because each weight is used for only one input channel. On an accelerator designed around dense convolution it can run at a few per cent of peak, so a network that is 4× cheaper in operations can be slower in wall-clock time. This is the clearest example in the field of why operation counts are not a performance metric, and it is a question worth asking any vendor: not ‘what is your TOPS’ but ‘what is your utilisation on a depthwise layer’.

How this is got wrong. By quoting the TOPS figure and stopping. The array is 131 TOPS and the memory system can feed it 1/1300th of what it wants. Every architectural feature of a real accelerator — the weight buffer, the activation buffer, the tiling, the fusion of adjacent layers — exists to close that factor of 1300, and the accelerator is really a memory system with multipliers attached. A design house pitching an accelerator should lead with the memory hierarchy, because that is what a knowledgeable buyer will ask about first.

The outlier problem, and why it changed transformer accelerators. The table above uses Gaussian weights, for which max-based scaling is reasonable. Real transformer activations are not Gaussian: a few channels carry values tens of times larger than the rest, and a per-tensor scale chosen to contain them leaves the remaining channels occupying two or three bits. The measured consequence is that naive int8 quantisation of a large language model loses far more accuracy than the same procedure applied to a convolutional network, and the remedies — per-channel and per-group scales, keeping outlier channels in higher precision, or migrating the scale between activations and weights — all cost hardware that a convolution-era accelerator does not have. An accelerator's quantisation support is a workload-specific design decision, not a generic feature.
Problem. Why does int8 inference usually retain accuracy while int8 training does not?
Solution. Because the two use the numbers differently. Inference computes a forward pass whose outputs pass through nonlinearities that are insensitive to small perturbations, and errors do not accumulate across iterations — each inference is independent. Training computes gradients, which are (a) small differences of large numbers, so relative precision matters far more, (b) accumulated over many steps, so bias accumulates exactly as Part X1's truncating accumulator did, and (c) spread over an enormous dynamic range, because gradients of different layers differ by orders of magnitude. The practical resolution is the asymmetric one: low-precision multiplies with higher-precision accumulation and master weights, plus loss scaling to move small gradients out of the denormal region. bfloat16 exists for exactly the (c) reason — it sacrifices mantissa, which inference cares about, to keep exponent range, which training cares about.
Problem. A customer wants your accelerator to be ‘future proof’. What can you honestly offer?
Solution. Not architectural coverage of models that do not exist. What you can offer is specific and worth more than a vague claim: a programmable dataflow rather than a fixed one, so a new layer shape maps at some efficiency rather than not at all; a well-specified fallback path to a host or a DSP for operators the array cannot do, with measured numbers for that path so the customer can compute the Amdahl penalty themselves; a compiler that is open enough to extend, since in practice the compiler ages faster than the silicon; and measured utilisation on a set of named, versioned models, so the customer can extrapolate from evidence rather than from adjectives. Offering the measurement methodology is itself a differentiator: most datasheets in this field quote one peak number and one optimistic model, and a buyer who has been burned once recognises the difference immediately.
