Y11. The Modelling Engineer’s Working Day

This book was written for someone joining a modelling team. This part describes the job concretely: what is produced, how it is judged, what the first ninety days look like, and the specific habits that distinguish a modelling engineer whose work is trusted from one whose work is checked.

The bit-accurate model is the deliverable the role is named for, and its value rests entirely on one property: independence. It must encode the specification's behaviour, not the design's. A model written by reading the RTL is a paraphrase, and a paraphrase agrees with the original for exactly the wrong reason. The practical discipline is to write it from the standard or the algorithm description before the RTL exists, and to resist the pressure — which is constant and comes from everyone — to ‘just make the model match’ when the two disagree near a deadline. Every time you make the model match, you delete a check that cannot be recovered, and nobody will remember later which checks were deleted.

Y11.2 Fixed point is most of the work

The floating-point algorithm is usually short. Turning it into something implementable is not, and that conversion is where a modelling engineer spends most of their time. Part X1 gives the arithmetic; this is the workflow.

Table 386. The six models, and what each one answers
StageModelQuestionOutput
1Floating-point referenceDoes the algorithm work at all?A plot against the metric
2Range analysisWhat does each node hold?Integer bits per node, with the basis stated
3Quantised model with parameterised word lengthsHow does the metric degrade?A sweep, as in Part X1
4Word-length optimisationWhat is the cheapest point that passes?A vector of word lengths and the cost model used

6Corner vectorsWhat must the RTL handle that random stimulus will not reach?A directed vector set
These are six models, not one model in six states. Keeping them separate means the floating-point reference remains available to answer ‘is the algorithm wrong or is the quantisation wrong?’ — a question that arises constantly and is unanswerable once the models have been merged.
Worked example 55. A disagreement, diagnosed properly
Given. The RTL and the bit-exact model differ on 3 samples in 100 000, always by one LSB, always when the input is near full scale.
Method. Do not guess. Use the model hierarchy: compare the bit-exact model against the quantised model, and the quantised against floating point, on the same three inputs. The stage where the discrepancy appears names the cause.

How this is got wrong. By deciding which is right from which is easier to change. The order of rounding and saturation is a real specification question with a real answer, and getting it wrong produces a block that differs from every other implementation by one LSB at full scale — which will be found by a customer comparing against a competitor. One-LSB disagreements are the ones most often waved away and most often genuine; the discipline is that a disagreement is closed by a citation to the specification, not by a judgement about magnitude.

Y11.3 The first ninety days

The pattern is deliberate: run before reading, read before writing, small before large, and process improvement last — because a process criticism from someone who has not yet shipped anything through the process is ignored, and the same criticism after ten weeks is acted on.
The failure mode of a new modelling engineer is silent disagreement. You read the specification, you believe the existing model is wrong, and you say nothing because you assume the experienced people have a reason. Sometimes they do, and hearing it is how you learn. Sometimes they do not, and the bug ships. The correct move is neither silence nor an accusation: it is a question with the clause number attached. ‘Clause 7.3.2 says the rounding happens before saturation and I read the model as doing the reverse — what am I missing?’ costs nothing if you are wrong and is extremely valuable if you are right. Engineers who ask this way get answers; engineers who assert get arguments.
Problem. You are asked to model a block whose specification is an internal document written by an architect who has left. What do you do?
Solution. Treat the gap as the first deliverable. Write down what the document does not determine — every place where two readings are possible, every constant with no stated basis, every behaviour on an illegal input — and circulate that list. It is immediately useful to everyone and it is the cheapest thing you can produce in week one. Then resolve the list by evidence in this order: existing RTL or a previous generation, since what shipped is a fact; test vectors or captured data, which settle behaviour without settling intent; the people who integrated against it, who know what they relied on; and finally a decision recorded as a decision, with your name and the date, for the items nothing resolves. What you must not do is make the choices silently inside the model. A model containing twenty undocumented decisions is not a reference; it is a second design, and when it disagrees with the RTL nobody will be able to say which is right.
Problem. Your model and the RTL agree perfectly on the first day of integration. Is that good news?
Solution. It is news that needs checking before it is good. Perfect agreement on day one has three explanations and only one of them is happy. The comparison may not be running — a checker that is disabled, a stimulus that never reaches the block, a comparison of two constants; this is the first thing to rule out, and the way to rule it out is to break something deliberately and confirm the comparison notices, which is the same self-check the agent in Part Y4 performs every round. The model may have been written from the RTL, in which case agreement is tautological. Or the block may genuinely be simple and correct, which does happen. Establishing which costs half an hour and is the single highest-value half hour in an integration, because everything downstream rests on the answer.
