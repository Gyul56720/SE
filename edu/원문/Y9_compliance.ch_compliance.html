Y9. Standards, Compliance and Interoperability

A protocol block is worth what it can be shown to interoperate with. That showing is a separate activity from verification, with its own vocabulary, its own costs and its own failure modes, and it is the part of the work that first-time vendors most consistently under-budget. This part lays it out.

Y9.1 Three different things called ‘testing’

Table 431. What each activity proves and what it does not
ActivityQuestion answeredWho runs itWhat it misses
VerificationDoes the block do what I specified?YouWhether your reading of the standard is right
ConformanceDoes the block do what the standard says?You, or a test house, against a published test suiteWhether real partners interoperate — conformant implementations can still fail together
InteroperabilityDoes it work with this specific partner?A plugfest, or a lab with partner equipmentPartners not present; the next revision
CertificationWill the body let me use the logo?An accredited laboratoryEverything above — it is a process outcome, not a technical one
Two conformant implementations can fail to interoperate, and the reason is structural rather than anyone's fault. Standards contain optional features, permitted ranges and ambiguous sentences. If your block implements an option and the partner does not, both conform and neither works with the other unless the negotiation that selects it is itself implemented correctly on both sides — and negotiation code is under-tested everywhere, because testing it requires a partner that behaves differently from you. The practical consequence is that the negotiation and fallback paths deserve more verification effort than the steady-state datapath, which is the opposite of where effort naturally goes. In every protocol in this book, the bugs found at plugfests are concentrated in link training, capability exchange and error recovery.

Worked example 70. Whether to certify at all
Given. A block that could be sold with or without a logo. Certification costs $60 000 and eight weeks. Without it you expect 4 customers over three years at $80 000; with it you expect 7, and you can charge $95 000 because the logo removes a risk from the buyer's evaluation.
Method. Compute both revenues net of cost and time. Then note the factor the arithmetic does not contain.
Revenue without certification320,000 USD
Revenue with certification665,000 USD
Certification cost60,000 USD
Eight weeks of engineering at $120k/yr18462 USD
Net with certification5.8654×105 USD
Net without320,000 USD
Difference2.6654×105 USD
How this is got wrong. By treating the customer counts as data. They are forecasts, and the forecast with certification is the one you have least evidence for, since it is the counterfactual. The robust way to use this calculation is not to trust its output but to find the break-even customer count and ask whether it is plausible: here certification pays if it brings even one additional customer at the higher price. Framed that way the decision is usually easy, and the arithmetic has done its job by converting an argument about strategy into a question about one number.

The interoperability list is a sales document as much as an engineering one. ‘Tested against these seven implementations, at these configurations, on these dates’ answers the question a buyer cannot otherwise answer about a supplier they do not know, and it answers it with something checkable. It also has a defensive function: when a customer reports an interoperability failure with an eighth partner, the list makes it immediately clear that this is new territory rather than a regression, which changes the conversation entirely. Keep it current and date every row; a list without dates is read as a list of claims rather than of tests.
Problem. A partner at a plugfest insists your implementation is wrong. You believe theirs is. How do you proceed?
Solution. Stop arguing and go to the text, together. Find the clause, read it aloud, and establish what each implementation does at that point in the exchange with a capture rather than with an assertion. Three outcomes follow and all three are useful. The text is clear and you are wrong: fix it, and you have discovered a real bug cheaply. The text is clear and they are wrong: show them, courteously, and consider whether to interoperate with their behaviour anyway — if they ship in volume, your customers will meet them, and a conformant block that fails against a popular partner is a commercial problem whoever is right. The text is ambiguous: this is the most valuable outcome, because it means everyone has the problem. Raise it with the working group; a clarification with your name on it is reputation, and it is one of the few ways a small house becomes visible to the people who buy. In all three cases the conduct matters more than the verdict: plugfests are small worlds and the engineer across the table is a future customer, partner or employer.
Problem. Why should a soft-IP vendor care about compliance at all, when the customer's chip is what gets certified?
Solution. Because the customer's certification failure becomes your problem regardless of whose name is on the certificate. Practically, three things follow. Your block must be testable in their chip: the compliance tests require modes — loopback, pattern generation, error injection, counter read-out — that must exist in the RTL and be reachable from their software, and adding them late is expensive. You must supply the evidence they will be asked for: which clauses your block implements, which options, which are the customer's responsibility. And you should have run what you can run yourself, because a block that has never seen a compliance suite will fail one, and it is far cheaper to fail it in your lab than in theirs. A vendor who arrives with a clause-by-clause implementation statement and their own test results is selling a lower-risk product, and that is precisely what the premium in protocol IP is paid for.
