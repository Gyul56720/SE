X43. Power Intent: Domains, Retention and Isolation

Power gating is the only technique that attacks leakage to near zero, and it is the one that changes an IP block's interface, its reset behaviour and its verification plan. A vendor whose block supports it properly is selling something materially different from one whose block does not.

Worked example 42. Is retention worth it, or should the block re-initialise?
Given. A block with 20 000 flops, of which 3 000 hold configuration and 17 000 hold datapath state. Leakage when on is 12 mW. The alternative to retention is for software to rewrite the configuration on wake.
Method. Compare three options on area, wake latency and residual leakage. Retention flops still leak, because their storage is always on.

How this is got wrong. By retaining everything because it is simplest. Datapath state is usually worthless across a power-down — the block was idle, so there is nothing in flight — and retaining it costs area and leakage for nothing. The design work is to partition state into what must survive and what must not, and then to make the latter's reset unconditional so that a wake-up is indistinguishable from a cold start. That partition is a specification item and is the single most useful thing a vendor can document about power behaviour.

X43.2 What UPF adds, and why RTL alone is not enough

Why power intent lives outside the RTL
StepJustification
1RTL describes function; it has no notion of a supply.A Verilog module does not say which rail powers it.
2Power domains, switches, isolation and retention are structural and depend on the floorplan and the process.The same RTL is used with and without power gating in different products.
3So they are described in a separate file — UPF or CPF — that the tools read alongside.Synthesis inserts the cells, verification simulates the power states, and place-and-route builds the switch network.
4Simulation must then model a powered-down domain as X, and check that isolation is enabled before power goes away.This is the verification that cannot be done in RTL alone, and it is where the bugs are.
5The power state table enumerates the legal combinations of domain states.And the illegal ones are what must be proven unreachable — a formal problem of exactly the kind Part X33 recommends.

The legal column assumes a simple nested hierarchy — each domain requires the ones below it. The transition column is the verification burden, and it is quadratic, which is why power-state machines are kept deliberately simple and why a block that demands its own independent domain is expensive to integrate.
The classic power-gating bug is an isolation enable that arrives too late, and it is not a functional bug in the usual sense. If power is removed before isolation is enabled, the outputs float for a short interval; downstream gates in the always-on domain then sit at an intermediate level and conduct, drawing a current spike that is invisible to functional simulation and shows up as excess power or, in the worst case, as damage. The sequence is: assert isolation, assert retention save, then remove power — and the reverse on wake. Getting it right is a small state machine; proving it right requires power-aware simulation, and asserting it is what a UPF-aware formal check does. An IP block that supplies its own power-control sequence and the assertions that check it is removing a whole class of integration risk.

Problem. Why is ‘race to idle’ sometimes better than running slowly, when X43.2 shows energy per operation is lowest at low voltage?
Solution. Because the block is not the only thing consuming power. Running at the top operating point finishes the work sooner and lets the whole subsystem — memory, interconnect, PLLs, the always-on logic, and any peripheral waiting on the result — enter a low-power state earlier. That static and shared component does not scale with the block's voltage, so the comparison is between (fast, high dynamic, short time, then everything off) and (slow, low dynamic, long time, everything on throughout). Race to idle wins when the fixed overhead per unit time is large relative to the block's own dynamic power, which is common in small IP blocks inside large systems and uncommon in a block that dominates its chip. The crossover is computable from the block's dynamic power at each point, the platform's idle power, and the entry/exit energy of the low-power state — and it is worth computing rather than asserting, because both answers appear in the literature and both are right for their own systems.
