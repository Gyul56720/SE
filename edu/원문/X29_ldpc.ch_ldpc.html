X29. LDPC and Polar Codes, Worked

These are the codes that reach within a fraction of a decibel of capacity and they are, between them, the error correction of 5G, Wi-Fi, high-speed Ethernet and modern storage. Their hardware is dominated by memory and interconnect rather than arithmetic, which makes them an unusually good case study in the gap between an algorithm's operation count and its silicon.

X29.1 Belief propagation, and the approximation everyone ships

From the check equation to the min-sum rule
StepJustification
1A check node enforces that the XOR of its connected bits is zero.Parity check.
2The log-likelihood ratio of a XOR of independent bits satisfies tanh(L/2) = Π tanh(Li/2).The standard identity: probabilities of a parity multiply in the tanh domain.
3So the exact check update is L = 2 tanh−1(Π tanh(Li/2)).Accurate and unimplementable: a transcendental function per edge, at hundreds of gigabits per second.
4Because tanh saturates, the product is dominated by its smallest term.Every factor with large |L| is close to ±1.

The scaling and offset constants cost one multiplier or one subtractor in the whole decoder and recover a measurable part of the loss; this is the cheapest improvement available in an LDPC decoder and it is universal in shipping designs.
Why the all-ones codeword is a legitimate test and not a shortcut. For a linear code over a symmetric channel, the error probability is independent of the transmitted codeword: the decoder's behaviour depends only on the noise realisation relative to the sent word, and the code's symmetry makes every word equivalent. So simulating the all-zero (or all-ones, under the sign convention used here) word is exact, not approximate, and it removes the encoder from the simulation entirely. The condition to check is that the decoder is genuinely symmetric — a quantised implementation with an asymmetric saturation or a biased rounding rule is not, and then the shortcut becomes invalid. That is a real trap in fixed-point LDPC verification and a good reason to run a full encoder at least once against the shortcut.

The edge count sets everything. A decoder is a memory with comparators attached: the message memory dominates area, and moving those messages between the variable and check phases dominates power. Arithmetic — comparisons and adds — is almost free by comparison.

Why quasi-cyclic codes are the ones that get standardised. A quasi-cyclic parity-check matrix is built from circulant blocks — identity matrices shifted by a stored amount. Three consequences follow, and all three are hardware consequences rather than coding ones. The matrix is described by a small table of shift values instead of a full incidence list, so the code fits in a few hundred bytes of ROM. A whole block-row can be processed in parallel with perfectly regular addressing, because a circulant is a barrel shifter. And the same hardware serves many block lengths by changing the circulant size, which is what lets one decoder cover a standard's whole rate and length family. The structure exists for the decoder's benefit, at a small cost in threshold performance, and that trade is why every modern LDPC standard is quasi-cyclic.

X29.3 Polar codes, and why they won the control channel

Channel polarisation in one page
StepJustification
1Combine two uses of a channel W as u1⊕u2 and u2.The basic 2×2 transform.
2Decoding u1 first sees a worse channel; decoding u2 given u1 sees a better one.The mutual information is conserved but redistributed: I(W−) + I(W+) = 2I(W).
3Recurse n times over 2n uses.The fraction of synthetic channels that are nearly perfect tends to I(W) and the rest tend to useless.
4Put information on the good channels and freeze the rest to known values.That is the entire code construction — no search, no optimisation, and it provably achieves capacity as the length grows.
5Decoding is successive cancellation: decide bits in order, using earlier decisions.Inherently serial, which is the hardware problem; latency grows with block length even though the operation count is NlogN.
6Adding a CRC and keeping L candidate paths (SCL) closes most of the gap to maximum likelihood.At L× the memory and the sorting network, which is what 5G specifies for control channels.

Latency is the polar decoder's problem and list size is its area problem. The published architectural work is almost entirely about shortening the serial schedule — recognising sub-blocks that can be decoded in one step rather than bit by bit.
Problem. 5G uses polar codes for control channels and LDPC for data. Why not one code for both?
Solution. Because the two channels have opposite requirements and each code is better at one of them. Control messages are short — tens of bits — and at those lengths LDPC's performance degrades badly, because the Tanner graph cannot avoid short cycles and the asymptotic analysis that makes LDPC excellent does not apply. Polar codes with CRC-aided list decoding are close to optimal at short lengths, and their error detection is excellent, which matters enormously for control: a mis-decoded control message is worse than a detected failure, exactly as Part X5 argued for Reed–Solomon. Data blocks are long and need throughput, and there LDPC's parallel decoding wins decisively while polar's serial successive cancellation would set a latency floor. The standard is not being indecisive; it is applying the right tool at each length, and the lesson generalises: code choice is dominated by block length and by whether detection or throughput is the binding requirement.
