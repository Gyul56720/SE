X25. Detection and Estimation, the Receiver’s Mathematics

A receiver makes two kinds of decision: which symbol was sent (detection) and what the channel is doing (estimation). Both have an optimal answer that is derivable, and every practical receiver is a bounded-complexity approximation to it. Knowing the optimum is what lets you say how much a shortcut costs.

X25.1 The likelihood ratio is the whole of detection

From a cost to the optimal decision rule
StepJustification
1Two hypotheses H0, H1 with priors P0, P1; observation r.The setup for any binary decision.
2Minimising the probability of error means choosing the hypothesis with the larger posterior P(Hi|r).Each wrong choice costs one; pick the more likely.
3By Bayes, this is p(r|H1)P1 ≷ p(r|H0)P0.The evidence term cancels.
4Λ(r) = p(r|H1) / p(r|H0) ≷ P0/P1The likelihood ratio test. Every optimal detector is this; only the densities change.
5For additive white Gaussian noise the log of Λ is linear in r, giving a matched filter followed by a threshold.Take logs of the Gaussian density: the quadratic terms that depend on r alone cancel in the ratio, leaving an inner product. This is why the matched filter is optimal and not merely intuitive.
6Working with logΛ rather than Λ turns products into sums.Which is why hardware carries log-likelihood ratios: combining independent observations becomes addition, and the dynamic range collapses from astronomical to a few bits.

X25.2 Soft information: why a hard decision throws away 2 dB

A slicer that outputs only a bit discards the receiver's confidence. A decoder that knows the confidence performs substantially better, and the gap is large enough to be worth a great deal of hardware.

The same received samples decoded two ways. Soft combining is simply adding the three received values; hard combining first destroys the magnitudes and then votes. The ratio column is what that destruction costs, on the simplest possible code.
The classical figure of about 2 dB. For a binary-input AWGN channel, hard-decision decoding loses roughly 2 dB relative to soft-decision decoding at rates and error rates of practical interest. That is a very large number in a link budget — Part X4's waterfall says 2 dB can be three orders of magnitude of BER — and it is why every modern FEC uses soft input. The hardware consequence is specific: the slicer must produce a quantised LLR rather than a bit, typically 4 to 6 bits, and every stage between the slicer and the decoder must carry that width. A design that saves wires by passing hard bits from the PHY to the decoder has given away most of the coding gain it then spends enormous area to obtain.

X25.3 Estimation: the bound that tells you when to stop improving

The Cramér–Rao bound
StepJustification
1For an unbiased estimator of a parameter θ from data with density p(r;θ), var(θ̂) ≥ 1/I(θ).The Cramér–Rao inequality.
2I(θ) = E[(∂ln p/∂θ)2] is the Fisher information.How sharply the likelihood peaks — a flat likelihood means the data barely constrain θ.
3For N independent observations, I scales with N.So the standard deviation falls as 1/√N, and no estimator does better. Quadrupling the averaging halves the error, always.
4For frequency estimation from N samples the bound scales as N−3/2.Much faster than 1/√N, because a frequency error accumulates phase over the observation, so a longer observation is disproportionately informative. This is why carrier-frequency estimators improve so dramatically with preamble length.
5An estimator that meets the bound is efficient.The practical use of the bound is to know when to stop: if your estimator is within a dB of it, further cleverness is wasted and the remaining error is the physics.

Problem. Your carrier-frequency estimator is 3 dB from the Cramér–Rao bound. Is it worth improving?
Solution. Ask what the 3 dB buys downstream, not whether 3 dB is a lot. Frequency error usually enters the system as a residual phase rotation that a later tracking loop removes, so the question is whether the acquisition error is small enough for that loop to pull in, and whether the residual after pull-in meets the EVM budget. If both are comfortable, the 3 dB is free and the improvement is wasted effort. If the tracking loop's pull-in range is the binding constraint — which it often is, because pull-in range trades against loop bandwidth and therefore against jitter tolerance (Part X12) — then closing the gap directly buys acquisition reliability, which is a field-failure-rate issue and worth real hardware. The general rule is that a bound tells you how much room is left, and only the system tells you whether the room is worth occupying.
Problem. Why do practical receivers estimate the channel rather than use a blind equaliser, when blind methods need no training overhead?
Solution. Because training overhead is cheap and convergence risk is not. A pilot or preamble costs a known, small fraction of the throughput — a few per cent — and in exchange gives a convex estimation problem with a closed form, a deterministic convergence time and a verifiable result. Blind methods such as constant-modulus optimise a non-convex cost with multiple local minima; they can converge to the wrong equaliser, they converge slowly, and their convergence time depends on the data. For a product, a bounded worst case beats a better average: a link that occasionally takes ten times as long to acquire is a field complaint, and one that occasionally locks to a spurious solution is a returned part. Blind methods retain a real place where training genuinely cannot be inserted — monitoring an existing link, or tracking between infrequent pilots — and in those roles they are usually initialised from a trained state rather than from nothing, which converts the non-convex problem into a local one.
