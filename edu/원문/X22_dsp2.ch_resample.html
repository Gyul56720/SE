X22. Resampling and Timing Recovery, Worked

Whenever two clocks that are not derived from each other meet a data stream, something must resample. In an audio codec it is a sample-rate converter; in a receiver it is the timing-recovery interpolator; in a video pipeline it is a scaler. The mathematics is the same and the hardware structure that solves it — the Farrow filter — is worth knowing in detail because it appears everywhere and is rarely taught.

X22.1 The problem: a sample that was never taken

Interpolation as filtering, and why a fixed filter is not enough
StepJustification
1An ideal band-limited signal can be reconstructed exactly from its samples by convolution with a sinc.Sampling theorem.
2So the value at any intermediate instant μ is a weighted sum of neighbouring samples, with weights sinc(k − μ).Evaluate the reconstruction at that instant. The weights depend on μ.
3For a fixed μ this is one FIR filter, and a set of L fixed filters gives L discrete phases.The polyphase interpolator: fine when the phase set is known in advance.
4Timing recovery needs a μ that varies continuously and is not known in advance.The sampling phase drifts with the frequency offset between transmitter and receiver, by an amount nobody can tabulate.
5Farrow's insight: approximate each weight as a polynomial in μ, hk(μ) = Σck,mμm.Then the interpolator becomes M+1 fixed FIR filters whose outputs are combined by Horner's rule in μ.
6The coefficients are constant; only μ changes.So the multipliers can be constant-coefficient, and a continuously variable delay costs M general multiplies rather than a new filter per phase.

Worked example 20. How much oversampling does a link need for its interpolator?
Given. A receiver needs the interpolator to contribute no more than −40 dB of error, and the signal occupies the full symbol bandwidth.
Method. Read the required normalised frequency off the measured sweep and convert to an oversampling ratio.

How this is got wrong. By choosing the oversampling ratio from the sampling theorem alone. Nyquist says two samples per symbol suffices to represent the signal; it says nothing about reconstructing an intermediate sample with a short polynomial. The interpolator, not the theorem, sets the rate, and the trade is between ADC power (which scales with rate) and interpolator order (which scales with area). Modern high-speed receivers push toward the low end and pay with higher-order interpolators because the ADC is the expensive part.

Why the integral term is not optional, stated precisely. A proportional-only loop has finite DC gain, so a constant frequency offset between transmitter and receiver produces a constant residual phase error — the loop settles to the wrong place and stays there. Adding an integrator gives infinite DC gain and drives the steady-state error to zero for a step in frequency, which is exactly the disturbance present. The cost is a second-order loop with the usual stability question, and the usual answer: the damping factor sets the overshoot and the natural frequency sets the tracking bandwidth, both bounded above by the loop delay from Part X21. Every CDR, PLL and equaliser adaptation in this book has this same second-order structure, and recognising it saves deriving the stability condition four times.
Problem. Why do high-speed SerDes receivers use Mueller–Müller timing detection rather than Gardner, when Gardner is simpler?
Solution. Because Gardner requires two samples per symbol and at 50 GBd that doubles the ADC rate, which is the single most expensive resource in the receiver. Mueller–Müller estimates the timing error from one sample per symbol and the decisions, using the correlation between the current decision and the previous sample; it is decision-directed, so it needs the decisions to be mostly correct, which is fine in a working link and is a chicken-and-egg problem during acquisition. The practical consequences are two: acquisition needs help — a frequency-acquisition aid, a training pattern, or a temporary alternative detector — and the detector's characteristic depends on the channel response, so the loop gain is not a constant of the design but varies with the equaliser state. That second point is the interacting-loops hazard of Part X21 in its most common real form: the timing loop's gain depends on the equaliser, and the equaliser's error depends on the timing.
