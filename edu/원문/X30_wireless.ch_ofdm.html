X30. OFDM and MIMO, Worked

Every wireless standard of the last twenty years is OFDM, and most are MIMO. Both are, from a hardware point of view, ways of converting a hard problem into a large number of easy ones — and the conversion has costs that are computable and are the subject of most of the implementation literature.

X30.1 Why OFDM turns equalisation into division

The cyclic prefix is the whole trick
StepJustification
1A multipath channel convolves the transmitted signal with an impulse response of length L.Linear convolution.
2Circular convolution in time is multiplication in the DFT domain.The convolution theorem — but only for circular convolution.
3A channel performs linear convolution, which is not circular, so the theorem does not apply.The blocks bleed into each other.
4Prepend the last L−1 samples of each block to itself.Now the tail that bleeds in is the same as what would have wrapped around, so over the retained window the linear convolution is circular.
5Therefore Yk = HkXk + Nk, one complex multiply per subcarrier.Equalisation becomes one complex division per subcarrier, replacing a time-domain equaliser of length L. This is the entire reason OFDM exists.
6Cost: the prefix is redundancy, so the rate falls by N/(N+L−1).And the peak-to-average ratio rises, because a sum of many independent subcarriers is nearly Gaussian — X30.2.

Computed by transforming random QPSK symbols. The theoretical worst case is never approached — it needs all subcarriers to align in phase, which has vanishing probability — but the 99.9th percentile grows slowly and is the number the power amplifier must be backed off by.
PAPR is a power-amplifier problem, and the power amplifier is the most expensive and least efficient thing in a radio. An amplifier must be operated below its compression point by the PAPR or the peaks clip, generating out-of-band emission that violates the spectral mask. Backing off 8 dB means operating at a small fraction of the amplifier's capability, and amplifier efficiency falls steeply with back-off — which in a handset is battery life and in a base station is the electricity bill and the cooling. This is why PAPR reduction (clipping and filtering, tone reservation, selected mapping) and digital pre-distortion exist, and why single-carrier FDMA was chosen for the LTE uplink: the handset's amplifier is the constraint, so the uplink gave up OFDM's convenience to get several dB of PAPR back. An architectural decision in a standard, driven entirely by one analogue component's efficiency curve.

X30.3 MIMO: capacity, and the detector that costs it

Why multiple antennas multiply capacity
StepJustification
1With Nt transmit and Nr receive antennas, y = Hx + n.H is Nr×Nt complex.
2Decompose H = UΣVH; the channel becomes min(Nt,Nr) parallel scalar channels with gains σi.The singular value decomposition of Part X24, used as a change of basis.
3Capacity = Σ log2(1 + Piσi2/N0).Sum of parallel Gaussian channels, with power allocated by water-filling.
4So capacity grows linearly with min(Nt,Nr), not logarithmically with power.This is the whole point: spatial multiplexing buys rate that more power cannot.
5It requires the σi to be comparable — a well-conditioned H.Line-of-sight channels are ill conditioned and give almost no multiplexing gain. Rich scattering, which sounds like a bad channel, is what makes MIMO work — and Part X24's condition number is again the deciding quantity.

Problem. Why does massive MIMO make the detector easier rather than harder, despite having far more antennas?
Solution. Because when Nr is much larger than Nt the columns of H become nearly orthogonal — a consequence of concentration of measure, since independent high-dimensional random vectors are nearly orthogonal with high probability. Nearly orthogonal columns mean HHH is nearly diagonal, so the simple matched filter (conjugate beamforming) is nearly optimal and the noise enhancement that makes zero forcing bad at low SNR essentially vanishes. The hard detector problem is a symptom of having barely enough antennas, and adding antennas at the base station — where size and power are affordable — dissolves it. The cost moves elsewhere and should be named: channel estimation for many antennas needs pilot resources and becomes the limiting factor (pilot contamination), the number of RF chains dominates cost and power, and the digital front end must move enormous sample rates from the antennas to the processing. Massive MIMO trades a detection problem for an interconnect and calibration problem, which is a trade the digital designer should recognise as favourable and should be ready to quantify.
