X16. Reliability, Yield and Functional Safety, Worked

Three quantities decide whether a chip can be sold into an automotive, industrial or medical market, and all three are arithmetic: the fraction of dies that work, the rate at which working dies stop working, and the fraction of dangerous failures that are detected. A design house that can compute them can quote for markets that pay considerably more than consumer.

X16.1 Yield: the model and its parameter

From defect density to die yield
StepJustification
1Defects fall on the wafer at an average density D0 per unit area.Empirical; the foundry supplies it and it falls over a process's life.
2If defects were Poisson and independent, yield would be e−AD0.Probability of zero defects in area A.
3They are not independent — defects cluster — so Poisson is pessimistic for large dies.Clustering means some wafers are bad and others clean, which raises the average yield of the clean ones.
4The negative-binomial (Murphy/Seeds) model adds a clustering parameter α: Y = (1 + AD0/α)−α.α ≈ 2–5 in practice; α → ∞ recovers Poisson.
5Yield falls superlinearly with die area, and total good dies per wafer falls faster still.Which is the whole argument for chiplets, and the reason a large monolithic die is a business decision as much as a technical one.

Worked example 9. Redundancy in a memory, and when it pays
Given. A 16 Mbit SRAM occupying 20 mm². Adding 2 % area of spare rows and columns with laser or fuse repair can repair any single defect in a row or column.
Method. Compare the yield of the memory with and without repair, using the negative-binomial model, and weigh the 2 % area against the yield gain.
Memory area20 mm²
Yield without repair0.9803
With 2 % area added0.9799
Defects repairable (assume 80 % are single row/column)80 %
Effective yield with repair0.996
Gain0.016
Area cost2 %
How this is got wrong. By applying the same reasoning to random logic. Memory repair works because memory is regular: a spare row is interchangeable with any row, so one spare covers a whole class of defects. Random logic has no such structure, and the equivalent — triple modular redundancy — costs over 200 % rather than 2 %. This asymmetry is why yield-enhancement effort concentrates on memory, and why a design that is 60 % memory by area has a very different yield story from one that is 60 % logic. It is worth knowing which yours is.

This is how a fifteen-year automotive lifetime is qualified in weeks: stress at 150 °C and multiply. The activation energy is per mechanism and is the parameter the whole extrapolation rests on; quoting a lifetime without naming Ea and the mechanism it was measured for is not a lifetime claim.
Acceleration factors multiply the error in Ea enormously. At 150 °C the factor above is roughly 170; had Ea been 0.5 eV instead of 0.7, it would be about 40. So a 10 % uncertainty in an exponent's parameter becomes a factor of four in the predicted lifetime, and the direction of the error is not conservative — assuming a high Ea makes the test look more severe than it was. This is the same structural hazard as the metastability τ of Part X3: a quantity inside an exponent, measured with modest precision, on which a large claim rests. Both deserve the same treatment: use the worst plausible value, and say which value you used.

How this is got wrong. By assuming safety is a verification activity. It is an architecture activity: 99 % coverage is not reachable by testing harder, it is reachable by building detection into the design, and detection costs area that must be budgeted at the start. The usual structure is a mixed one — duplication on the control path, where it is small and a fault is dangerous; ECC or parity on memories, where the regularity makes it cheap; and end-to-end checks such as a CRC over a datapath, where a single check covers a great deal of logic. A safety manual naming those mechanisms, with the coverage claimed for each, is a deliverable a customer pays for.
Problem. Why is a ‘latent’ fault treated separately, and why does it need periodic testing?
Solution. A latent fault is one in a safety mechanism rather than in the function: the comparator that would detect a duplication mismatch has itself failed stuck at ‘equal’. On its own it is harmless — the function still works — which is exactly why it is dangerous: nothing reveals it, so it accumulates, and when a real fault eventually occurs the detection that was counted on is absent. The metric that bounds this is LFM, and the mechanism that serves it is a periodic self-test: at start-up and at intervals during operation, deliberately inject a fault and confirm the detector fires. This is the same argument as the verification agent's self-check in Part Y4 and as this repository's whole rule about checking the checker — a detector that is never exercised is not known to work, and in a safety context that is not a style preference but a numbered requirement with a quantitative target.
