X7. Power and Energy, Worked

A design house is asked for three numbers about power: how much the block burns, how much of that is unavoidable, and what a customer can do about it. All three are computable, and a vendor who can produce them with their assumptions stated is selling something the competitor who quotes a single milliwatt figure is not.

Worked example 48. A gate-level power estimate from first principles
Given. A block with 200 000 gates, average switched capacitance 1 fF per gate, V = 0.8 V, f = 1 GHz, activity factor α = 0.15. Clock network adds 30 % and its α is 1.
Method. Apply αCV2f to the logic and to the clock separately, because their activity factors differ by nearly an order of magnitude and averaging them is the commonest source of a wrong estimate.
Logic dynamic power19200 mW
Clock network capacitance (30 % of logic)60000 pF
Clock dynamic power (α = 1)38400 mW
Clock as a fraction of total dynamic66.7 %
Total dynamic57600 mW
Energy per cycle57600 pJ
How this is got wrong. By applying one activity factor to the whole design. The clock toggles every cycle by definition; logic in a typical datapath toggles on ten to twenty per cent of cycles. An estimate that uses α = 0.2 uniformly understates the clock by 5× and, since the clock here is two thirds of the total, understates the block by a factor approaching two. Clock gating is the first power optimisation for exactly this reason, and its benefit is visible in this table before any tool is run.

The last column is the one that matters for a battery: energy per operation falls monotonically as the voltage drops, which is why near-threshold operation is attractive, while the frequency column says what it costs in throughput. Leakage is deliberately excluded here and X7.3 puts it back.
Parallelism converts the voltage curve into a genuine win, and this is the single most important architectural consequence of the table above. Suppose a block must deliver throughput T. Run one copy at 1.0 V, or two copies at the voltage that halves the frequency. The two copies have twice the capacitance but each switches half as often per unit of work, so dynamic power is 2 × (V′/V)2 × ½ = (V′/V)2 of the original. From the table, halving the frequency permits roughly a 0.65 V supply, giving about 0.42× the power for the same throughput at rather more than twice the area. This calculation is the reason modern chips are wide and slow rather than narrow and fast, and it is worth being able to do it on a whiteboard.

Computed from the subthreshold exponential alone; DIBL and gate leakage would steepen it further. The share column shows why a block that is a fifth leakage on the bench can be nearly half leakage in a hot enclosure.
Leakage and temperature form a positive feedback loop. Leakage raises power, power raises junction temperature, and higher temperature raises leakage exponentially. Below a certain thermal resistance the loop has a stable operating point; above it there is none, and the part runs away. This is not a theoretical concern in high-leakage processes: it is why datasheets specify a maximum junction temperature and a maximum case-to-ambient thermal resistance, and why a customer who improves cooling is not merely buying margin but may be buying stability. An IP vendor should state the leakage current at the maximum rated temperature, not at 25 °C.

Worked example 49. The savings are not additive — a worked composition
Given. A block is 100 mW: 60 mW clock, 25 mW logic, 15 mW leakage. Clock gating removes 60 % of clock power; multi-Vth removes 60 % of leakage; operand isolation removes 30 % of logic power.
Method. Apply each to its own term and re-total. Then compute what a naive addition of the three quoted percentages would have predicted.
Clock after gating24 mW
Logic after isolation17.5 mW
Leakage after multi-Vth6 mW
New total47.5 mW
Actual saving52.5 mW
Actual saving (%)52.5 %
Naive sum of the three quoted percentages150 %
Clock share before / after60% / 50.5%
How this is got wrong. By adding percentages that are percentages of different things. Each technique reduces one term, and after it has done so that term is a smaller share, which is why the second technique applied always returns less than its headline. The corollary is a procedure: always attack the largest term first, re-profile, and only then choose the next technique. A power plan written as a list of techniques without a breakdown to apply them to is not a plan.
Problem. A customer reports that your block consumes 2× the datasheet figure. List the measurements that distinguish the possible causes, in the order you would make them.
Solution. 1. Compare activity factors. The datasheet figure was measured with some stimulus; ask what theirs is. A block specified on random data and used on highly correlated data — or the reverse — can differ by a factor of two with nothing wrong. This is the most common cause and the cheapest to check. 2. Check the clock-gating enables are actually asserting, by toggling coverage or by a scope on the gated clock; an integration that ties an enable high disables the largest single saving. 3. Split dynamic from leakage by measuring at two temperatures: leakage doubles over 40 °C or so and dynamic does not, so two points separate the terms. 4. Check the supply — a 10 % overvoltage is a 21 % dynamic increase. 5. Only then suspect the block. Note that the first four are questions about the customer's system, and asking them requires having recorded your own measurement conditions; a datasheet power figure without its stimulus, voltage and temperature is unfalsifiable and therefore worthless in exactly this conversation.
