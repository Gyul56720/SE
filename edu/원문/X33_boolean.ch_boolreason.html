X33. Boolean Reasoning: BDDs, SAT and Equivalence Checking

Three tools an IP designer uses every week — logic equivalence checking, formal property verification and automatic test pattern generation — are the same engine underneath: a decision procedure for Boolean formulas. Knowing what that engine can and cannot do explains why one proof finishes in seconds and another never finishes, which is otherwise the most frustrating thing about these tools.

Canonicity is the property that makes BDDs both magical and fragile. Two functions are equal if and only if their reduced ordered BDDs are the identical graph, so equivalence is a pointer comparison rather than a search. The price is that the size depends catastrophically on the variable order: the same function can be linear under one order and exponential under another, and finding the best order is itself NP-hard. The canonical example is a multiplier, whose BDD is exponential under every order — which is exactly why BDD-based equivalence checking fails on datapaths and why modern tools use AIGs plus SAT instead. When a tool reports that it is ‘building BDDs’ and then runs out of memory, this is what happened, and the remedy is a different engine rather than a bigger machine.

X33.2 Why SAT is fast even though it is NP-complete

What a modern SAT solver actually does
StepJustification
1Convert the circuit to CNF by Tseitin encoding: one variable per gate, a few clauses per gate.Linear in circuit size — no blow-up here, unlike naive expansion.
2Search by assigning variables, propagating implications, and backtracking on conflict.DPLL. The naive version is hopeless.
3On conflict, derive a new clause that explains it (clause learning) and add it.This is the single change that made SAT practical. The learned clause prunes an entire region of the search space, not just the current branch.
4Backjump non-chronologically to the level the conflict actually came from.Rather than undoing one decision at a time.
5Restart periodically, keeping the learned clauses.Escapes a bad region of the search while retaining what was learned.
6Result: industrial instances with millions of variables solve in seconds, while small random instances at the hardness threshold do not.Structure, not size, decides. Circuits have enormous structure; that is why circuit problems are the easy case and why a tool's runtime is unpredictable in the specific way engineers observe.

How this is got wrong. By assuming equivalence checking always works. It works because it finds internal equivalence points and cuts the problem into small cones; a transformation that destroys that correspondence — retiming across register boundaries, sequential optimisation, a multiplier architecture change — leaves the tool with one enormous cone and it fails. The practical consequence is a rule for the synthesis flow: turn on transformations that break equivalence checking only when you are prepared to verify the result another way, and know which ones they are before the schedule depends on them.

X33.3 Model checking: proving something about all time

Bounded model checking, and what ‘bounded’ costs
StepJustification
1Unroll the design's transition relation k times.Copy the combinational logic k times, chaining state.
2Assert the initial state and the negation of the property.If satisfiable, the assignment is a counterexample trace of length ≤ k.
3Hand the whole thing to SAT.A bug of depth ≤ k is found; a bug of depth k+1 is not.
4So BMC is a very good bug hunter and not a proof.This is the distinction that matters when reading a tool's report: ‘proven to depth 40’ is not ‘proven’.
5A full proof needs induction, interpolation or IC3/PDR, which find an inductive invariant.These do give unbounded proofs, and they are what a tool means by ‘proven’ without a depth.
6The bound needed for confidence is the design's diameter — how many cycles to reach any reachable state.Usually unknown, and for a design with a large counter it is astronomically large. Which is why counters are abstracted before proving anything around them.
The three ways a formal proof lies to you, in order of frequency. Over-constraint: an assumption excludes the input that would have failed, so the property is proven about a machine that is not yours. This is the formal equivalent of a disabled checker and is by far the commonest; the defence is to check that the constraints are satisfiable together with interesting behaviour, which good tools report as ‘assumption coverage’. Vacuity: the property's antecedent never occurs, so it holds trivially — ‘if a grant is given without a request…’ proven because no grant is ever modelled. A bounded result read as a proof, as above. All three report green, which is why this book's rule applies with full force here: a proof whose assumptions have not themselves been checked is an unexamined green light.
Problem. Where should a design house spend its formal effort, given that it cannot afford to prove everything?
Solution. On the properties whose failure is catastrophic and whose state space is deep and narrow, which is where formal has the advantage over simulation. In practice that means, in order: arbiters and any structure that can starve, because starvation is a liveness property and simulation can never prove its absence; FIFOs, credit counters and flow control, where overflow and underflow are provable and the bugs appear only at full rate; protocol interfaces, where a vendor-supplied assertion set exists and proving compliance at the boundary is worth more than any amount of traffic; clock-domain crossing structures, where the interesting cases are timing-related and simulation is blind to them; and security properties, where the question is whether information can flow between two places and the answer must hold for all inputs. What not to spend it on: datapath equivalence at the algorithm level — a bit-exact reference model plus random simulation gets there faster — and anything with a large counter in the cone, until the counter has been abstracted.
