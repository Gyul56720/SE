Y1. MIPI CSI-2 and DSI, Worked as a Deliverable

Every phone camera, most automotive cameras and a large fraction of embedded vision sensors speak CSI-2; every phone display and most embedded panels speak DSI. The two share a PHY and a packet structure, which is why a design house that builds one has most of the other. This part works the numbers a customer will ask for, and states where the answers come from.

Worked example 60. Why the overhead is not a fixed percentage
Given. A 1920×1080 frame, 12 bits per pixel, RAW12 packing. Each line is one long packet: 4 bytes of header, the payload, and 2 bytes of footer. Between lines the link may drop to low-power state and return, costing TLPX + THS-PREPARE + THS-ZERO + sync, taken here as 300 ns.
Method. Compute the per-line overhead in bytes and in time, then express it as a fraction. The fraction depends on the line length, which is why a single percentage cannot be right for every mode.

How this is got wrong. By quoting one efficiency figure for the whole product. The overhead is dominated by a per-line constant, so efficiency collapses for short lines: a narrow region-of-interest readout — exactly what a machine-vision customer does — can spend more time in transitions than in data. The design response is to stay in high-speed state across lines when the blanking interval is short, which the specification permits and which many controllers do not implement. That feature is worth naming in a datasheet because a competitor's block that lacks it will lose the ROI benchmark.

The 1 ms ULPS wake-up is a system-level number that routinely surprises integrators. A design that powers the link down between frames to save energy cannot wake it in a line blanking interval; it can only wake it in a frame interval, and only if the frame rate leaves a millisecond spare. 30 fps gives 33 ms per frame, so it fits; a 240 fps burst mode does not. Power state transitions have latencies that are three to five orders of magnitude apart — 50 ns for LPX, 1 ms for ULPS — and a power management policy that treats them as interchangeable will either waste energy or drop frames.

On quoting a specification you have not read. The table above is deliberately marked. A design house that builds a MIPI controller buys the specification; there is no substitute and no legitimate way around it. Writing formulae from secondary sources into RTL is how a block comes to fail interoperability at a plugfest, and the failure is expensive precisely because it is found late and in public. The correct entry in a project plan is a line item for the specification and for membership, not an assumption that the numbers can be inferred.

Y1.3 Packet structure, ECC and CRC — and why both exist

Why the header has ECC and the payload has CRC
StepJustification
1The short packet header is 4 bytes: data identifier, two word-count bytes, and one ECC byte.CSI-2 packet structure.
2The ECC is a Hamming code over the preceding 24 bits, giving single-error correction and double-error detection.From Part X10's bound: 24 data bits need r = 5 for correction, plus one overall parity, which fits in the byte with two bits spare.
3A corrupted header is unrecoverable at any higher layer, because the word count tells the receiver where the packet ends.A wrong length desynchronises the stream for the remainder of the frame. Correction, not detection, is therefore required here.
4The payload carries a 16-bit CRC and no correction.A corrupted pixel is one bad pixel; the frame continues. Detection lets the application decide, and correction would cost far more for far less.
5The asymmetry is a design principle, not a quirk of this standard.Spend correction where a failure is structural and detection where it is local. The same reasoning produced the parity-plus-reload answer for configuration memory in Part X10.
Problem. A customer reports occasional frame corruption that disappears when they reduce the lane rate. Your ECC error counter is zero and your CRC counter is non-zero. What does that tell you, and what would the opposite pattern tell you?
Solution. CRC errors without ECC errors mean the payload is being corrupted while the headers survive. That is a strong hint, because headers and payload travel the same wires but not the same way: headers occur at the start of a burst, just after the HS settle time, and are short; payload is long and continuous. Corruption that spares the header and hits the payload points at something that accumulates over a burst — clock drift or an under-specified THS-SETTLE window that is fine for the first bytes and drifts later, or an SSC/jitter interaction, or simply insufficient eye at the sampling point for long runs. The opposite pattern, ECC errors without CRC errors, would point at the transition: the receiver is mis-sampling the first bytes after leaving low-power state, which is the classic symptom of an incorrect settle-time programming or of a PHY that reports ready too early. The instrumentation that makes this diagnosis possible — separate, readable counters for header and payload errors, with the ability to capture the first failing packet — is a feature to design in, not a debug hack; it is also a strong differentiator in a datasheet.

Y1.4 What the repository's real CSI-2 source shows

The code appendix of this book reproduces 32,676 lines across 55 files of a working MIPI CSI-2 receiver written for FPGA fabric. It is worth reading for one reason above all: it shows how much of the block is not the protocol. Word alignment, lane de-skew, the byte-to-pixel unpacker and the clock-domain crossing to the pixel clock take more code than the packet decoder.

What a design house adds on top of an open-source receiver like this one. The open block demonstrates the datapath. What it does not contain is what a customer pays for: a register map with a defined reset state and an IP-XACT description; error counters and an interrupt controller; support for more than one virtual channel and for the data types the customer's sensor actually emits; a verification environment with the protocol checkers and a compliance test list; synthesis and timing closure at a specified frequency in a named process with reports; lint and CDC sign-off; and documentation that lets an integrator finish without calling. Each of those is weeks of work, and together they are the product. A design house that believes the RTL is the product will price the work at a fraction of its cost and deliver something a customer cannot integrate.
