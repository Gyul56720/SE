Y6. From a Paper to a Block, and Back to a Paper

Research papers are the cheapest source of architecture a design house has, and the most dangerous, because a paper's incentives are not a product's incentives. A paper must be novel; a product must be robust. A paper reports the configuration that worked; a product must survive the ones that did not. This part is a procedure for extracting value from the literature without inheriting its optimism — and, in its second half, for publishing without committing the same offence.

The recurring pattern in hardware papers is the unstated operating point. A result is reported at the configuration where the proposed technique wins, and the sweep that shows where it loses is absent. This is not usually dishonesty; it is page limits and the reviewing process. But it means the first thing to do with a promising technique is to reproduce the sweep the paper did not print, in your own model, and find the crossover. If the crossover is on the wrong side of your customer's operating point, the technique is a publication and not a product — and you have learned that in a week rather than a quarter. This repository's own record contains the mirror image of this mistake: a headline figure of ‘1/59 the area’ taken from the most favourable corner of a sweep, with the loss condition present elsewhere in the same document.
Worked example 67. Reproducing the missing sweep: a worked reading
Given. A paper claims an unrolled architecture removes a timing bottleneck and reports a 59× area advantage over the standard-cell baseline at one operating point.
Method. Identify the parameter the advantage depends on, sweep it, and find where the sign changes. Here the parameter is the number of taps that must be unrolled, which grows with channel loss; area grows as 2n.

The arithmetic above takes ten minutes and changes the conclusion from ‘adopt’ to ‘adopt for short channels only’, which is a completely different product decision. The general procedure is: find the exponential, and find where it eats the constant. Almost every architecture paper contains exactly one such exponential, and the paper's operating point is chosen just below where it bites.

Y6.2 The prior-art search, done so that it means something

This book's repository enforces a rule that a prior-art note must be committed before the code for a topic. The rule exists because the same failure occurred five times: choose a topic, build, measure, write, and only then discover the work already existed. The procedure below is what the rule requires.

Table 423. A prior-art note that is worth the time it takes
SectionContentThe failure it prevents
Closest prior workarXiv or DOI, year, and one sentence on what it did‘I could not find anything’, which is not a finding
How we differOne paragraph, specific, falsifiableA difference that evaporates under a reviewer's first question
Queries runThe literal search strings, at least three, with the databasesAn unrepeatable search
Where you have not lookedNamed: patents, a paywalled venue, a language, an industry body's documentsTreating absence of evidence as evidence of absence
Confidence of each citationFull text, abstract, listing, or snippetCiting something you have not read as though you had
Possibilities not yet eliminatedA listA false sense of completion
The confidence marker is the mechanically enforceable part and therefore the part worth automating. This repository requires every citation in a paper to carry, on the document's face, whether the author read the full text, the abstract, a listing entry, or only a search snippet; a gate checks the document against a ledger of citations and fails the build if they disagree. What the gate cannot check is whether the marker is honest, and the repository's notes say so explicitly. That is the correct division: the machine forces the claim to be stated, and a human is accountable for its truth. A process that pretends the machine can verify reading is worse than one that admits it cannot, because it invites everyone to stop looking.
Problem. You find a patent that appears to cover the architecture you were about to build. What are the possible responses, and which are realistic for a one-person house?
Solution. Six responses exist and only some are realistic. Design around: read the claims, not the abstract — the claims are the legal scope and are usually much narrower than the description — and find an element of the independent claim you can avoid. This is the normal answer and it is realistic. Check the status: the patent may have lapsed for unpaid maintenance fees, may never have been granted in your customers' jurisdictions, or may expire before your product ships; all are cheap to check and all are common. License it: realistic only if the holder licenses, which large semiconductor holders often do not to small parties. Challenge validity: not realistic — the cost exceeds a small house's annual revenue. Ignore it: not an option; wilful infringement carries enhanced damages, and your customer's legal review will find it anyway. Change topic: often the correct answer, and the reason the prior-art search comes first. The meta-point is that the patent is information, not a verdict, and reading the claims carefully is a skill worth acquiring because it converts a project-ending discovery into a design constraint about half the time.

Y6.3 Measuring so that the number survives a reviewer

The four rules below are this repository's response to four specific measurements that were reported and were wrong. They are stated as a checklist because each failure looked, at the time, like a result.

Table 424. Four checks before a number is said aloud
CheckWhat went wrong without itHow to do it
Did you measure the quantity you defined?A tool reported the longest path in the whole netlist; the quantity wanted was the longest path around a loop. Different number, same unitsIsolate the quantity — cut the loop, build the combinational equivalent, and measure that
Is the operating point healthy?A residual analysis was run at a point where the bit error rate was 0.49 — the link was not working, so every derived number was noiseSweep first, find a working point, then measure. Report the point
Is there an independent cross-check?A reconstruction double-counted a term; the error was invisible until the same quantity was computed a second wayClosed form, a second estimator, or a hand calculation — and print the difference
Have you killed the trivial explanations?‘Seven gates, zero mismatch’ — because at that operating point the equalizer taps were near zero and the loop was doing nothingWrite the list of boring reasons the number could look good, then measure each one away
If you cannot do all four, say which one you could not do. A number with a stated gap is usable by a reader, who can weigh it. A number presented as complete when it is not is a claim the reader cannot calibrate, and when it turns out to be wrong it costs more than the information was ever worth. This applies with full force inside a company: the colleague reading your slide has no way to know which checks you ran unless you say.
Problem. Your simulation shows a 3× improvement. What do you do before telling anyone?
Solution. Assume it is a bug, because at that magnitude it usually is, and spend an hour trying to prove it. Check the baseline actually runs: the commonest cause of a large speed-up is a baseline that is mis-configured, unoptimised, or silently failing, and comparing against a broken baseline is the strawman failure from Y6.1 committed against yourself. Check the units and the normalisation — per operation, per cycle, per joule and per second are four different claims. Check that both sides did the same work: a fast path that skips a case the baseline handles is not faster. Compute the improvement a second way, from first principles, and see whether 3× is even plausible given what changed; if the change removed 20 % of the operations, a 3× speed-up needs an explanation beyond the operation count. Only then look for the mechanism, and when you report it, report the mechanism rather than the multiplier — a number with an explanation survives scrutiny and a number without one does not.
Problem. Should a one-person design house publish at all?
Solution. Yes, and for reasons that are commercial rather than academic. A publication is evidence a customer can check before trusting a supplier they have never heard of, which is the central problem of a new house. It is defensive prior art: published material cannot later be patented against you, which is cheap insurance compared with a patent of your own. It forces the four checks above, because a reviewer will ask, and work that survives review is work you can quote in a datasheet. And it brings the people — collaborators, customers and the occasional acquirer read the literature of their field. The cost is real: a paper is several weeks, and a rejection is several more. The pragmatic compromise many small houses take is an arXiv preprint plus a workshop or industry conference talk, which captures most of the benefit at a fraction of the cost of a top-tier submission, and which is perfectly compatible with holding back the implementation details that are actually the product.
