X27. Design for Test: Scan, BIST and ATPG, Worked

Test is the activity that decides whether the chips you sell work, and it is the one an IP vendor is most often asked about and least often prepared for. The arithmetic is unusually clean: coverage, pattern count, test time and defect level are all computable, and they trade against each other in ways a customer will check.

X27.1 Why scan, and what it costs

From an untestable machine to a testable one
StepJustification
1A sequential circuit's state is reachable only through its inputs over many cycles.To test a fault deep in the state space you must first drive the machine there, which may take an unknown and enormous number of cycles.
2Test generation for sequential circuits is therefore intractable in general.The search is over input sequences, not input vectors.
3Scan converts every flop into a shift-register stage in test mode.Now any state is loadable in L shifts and any state is observable in L shifts.
4The combinational logic between flops is then testable as a pure combinational circuit.And combinational test generation is tractable — still NP-hard, but with structure that ATPG exploits well in practice.
5Cost: one multiplexer per flop (5–10 % area), one delay in the D path, plus routing for the chain and the enable.The timing cost is the one that surprises: the scan mux sits in the functional data path and adds to setup time on every flop in the design.

Worked example 24. What scan compression buys
Given. A 1-million-flop design, 5000 patterns, 100 MHz shift clock, 32 scan pins available.
Method. Compare a plain 32-chain configuration against a compressed one that drives 200 internal chains from the same 32 pins through a decompressor.
Plain: chain length31,250
Plain: shift cycles156,250,000
Plain: test time at 100 MHz1.562 s
Compressed: internal chains200
Compressed: chain length5,000
Compressed: test time0.25 s
Compression ratio6.25×
Tester cost saved at $0.05/s over 10M units0.6562 M USD
How this is got wrong. By evaluating compression as an area cost rather than a revenue item. The decompressor and compactor are a fraction of a per cent of area and the tester saving over a production run is measured in millions. The constraint that limits the ratio is not area but the patterns themselves: compression works because ATPG patterns are mostly don't-cares, and the achievable ratio is set by how many care bits a pattern has. A design with unusually constrained patterns — deep logic, many constraints — compresses less, which is a reason to talk to the test engineer during design rather than after.

X27.2 Fault models, and what each one misses

Table 283. Fault models in industrial use
ModelAssumesCatchesMisses
Stuck-atA node is permanently 0 or 1Gross defects: opens, shorts to railsAnything timing related; resistive defects
Transition / at-speedA node is slow to rise or slow to fallResistive opens, weak drivers, process marginalityNeeds two patterns and at-speed launch — costly

Cell-awareDefects inside a standard cellDefects the gate-level models cannot expressRequires transistor-level analysis per cell — the library vendor must supply it
Stuck-at coverage of 99 % is not 99 % of defects, and conflating them is the commonest error in a test discussion. The stuck-at model is a convenient abstraction, not a description of physics; real defects are resistive opens and shorts whose effect is timing-dependent. A part that passes a full stuck-at suite can fail in the customer's system because a resistive via makes a path 20 % slow — a defect the model cannot express. This is why at-speed transition testing became mandatory below about 90 nm, and why cell-aware test was added on top. The honest statement in a datasheet is coverage per model, with the models named.

Given. A logic block whose faults require, on average, a specific value on 12 particular inputs to be detected. Pseudo-random patterns set each input independently to 0 or 1.
Method. Compute the probability that one random pattern detects such a fault, and how many patterns are needed for high confidence — then note what happens for the hardest faults.

How this is got wrong. By reporting the average and ignoring the tail. Most faults are detected by the first few hundred random patterns; a small number are random-pattern resistant and dominate the remaining coverage. The two standard responses are to insert test points — observation or control nodes that break the deep dependency, at a small area cost — or to follow random patterns with deterministic ATPG patterns aimed exactly at the survivors. Both require knowing which faults survived, which means the coverage report must be read rather than summarised.
Problem. A customer asks for your IP's fault coverage. What do you actually give them, and what do you need from them?
Solution. You cannot give a single number, and explaining why is part of the answer. Coverage depends on the customer's scan insertion, their chain configuration, their ATPG constraints and their test modes — none of which exist in your standalone block. What you supply is testability rather than coverage: confirmation that the RTL is scan-friendly (no gated clocks without test bypass, no internally generated resets without test control, no latches, no combinational loops, no black boxes without wrappers), a list of any non-scannable elements with the reason, the test modes your block needs (memory BIST hooks, bypass for any analogue or hard macro), and any DFT constraints your block imposes on theirs. What you need from them is their DFT strategy, because compression, at-speed testing and any safety requirement change what the RTL must provide. Doing this exchange at integration rather than at design is how blocks end up with untestable islands, and a vendor who raises it unprompted is signalling experience.
