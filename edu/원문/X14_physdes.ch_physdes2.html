X14. Physical Design as the RTL Designer Sees It

A soft-IP vendor does not run place-and-route on the customer's chip, but the vendor's RTL determines whether that run succeeds. This part covers the physical facts that propagate back into RTL decisions, with the arithmetic that makes them checkable.

X14.1 Wire delay does not scale, and that changes architecture

Why a long wire gets relatively slower every generation
StepJustification
1A wire's distributed RC delay is 0.38rcL2 for resistance r and capacitance c per unit length.Elmore delay of a distributed line. Quadratic in length — the single most important fact in this chapter.
2Scaling reduces wire cross-section, so r rises roughly as 1/S2 while c stays roughly constant.Thinner and narrower conductor, same dielectric spacing ratio.
3A wire of fixed physical length therefore gets slower as the process shrinks.While gates get faster. The ratio moves against the wire every generation.
4Buffering breaks the quadratic: k equal segments give k·(L/k)2 = L2/k plus k buffer delays.Optimise over k and the delay becomes linear in L.
5So a long wire costs area and power, and it costs a fixed delay per millimetre that no amount of cleverness removes.Which makes distance an architectural quantity. A design whose blocks must exchange data every cycle across a chip is not implementable at high frequency, however good the RTL.

How this is got wrong. By assuming any module can talk to any module in one cycle. At 3 GHz a signal crosses a fraction of a large die per cycle, so a chip-spanning bus is physically impossible at that frequency and the architecture must be pipelined, hierarchical, or both. This is the physical origin of networks-on-chip: not a preference for packets, but the observation that a global broadcast wire no longer exists. For an IP vendor the practical consequence is to keep the block's internal critical paths local and to register every boundary signal, so the customer's floorplanner has somewhere to put the pipeline stages the distance demands.

Synthesis can delete your synchroniser. The two flops of a synchroniser are functionally redundant to a tool that believes the input is a normal signal: it may merge them, retime through them, or move one across a boundary, any of which destroys the resolution time the MTBF calculation of Part X3 depends on. The defences are to mark the flops with a synthesis attribute that forbids merging and retiming, to place them in a module the tool is told not to ungroup, and to check the gate-level netlist that they are still there — a check that can be automated and belongs in the sign-off list. A design that relies on the tool's good behaviour here has an MTBF nobody has verified.

The model is a heuristic, not a PDK number: difficulty grows as (1−u)−1.5. Its only job is to make the shape visible — the cost is mild to 70 % and then rises very fast, which is why floorplans target 65–75 % and why ‘we will just raise utilisation’ is rarely the answer to an area problem.
Congestion is caused by pin density and connectivity, not by cell area, and this distinction decides what an RTL designer can do about it. A block that is 60 % utilised can be unroutable if its cells are small and richly connected — a crossbar, a large multiplexer tree, a register file read network — because the routing demand per unit area is set by nets crossing a boundary, not by transistors under it. The RTL-level remedies are therefore about connectivity: reduce the radix of a switch and add a stage; replace a fully-connected structure with a hierarchical one; move a wide mux into a memory where the decoder is dense and local; and avoid the one-hot broadcast structures that look cheap in gate count and are expensive in tracks. An area report will not show any of this; a congestion map will.
Problem. Your block meets timing standalone at 800 MHz but the customer reports it fails at 600 MHz in their chip. Name four causes, in the order you would check them.
Solution. 1. Different constraints. Your standalone run may have used ideal clocks, no input/output delay, or a different corner set. Ask for their SDC and compare it with yours line by line; this is the cause more often than the other three together. 2. Boundary paths. Standalone runs frequently constrain inputs and outputs generously; in the chip your block's inputs arrive late and its outputs must arrive early. This is why registering every boundary is worth the latency — it makes your timing independent of their floorplan. 3. Physical context. In their chip your block may be stretched around a macro, sit far from its clock source, or share power rails with a noisy neighbour, all of which add delay your standalone run did not model. 4. A different library or corner. Same process, different vendor library or different Vt mix, and your critical path is no longer their critical path. The general lesson for a vendor is to ship the constraints and the conditions with the frequency claim; a frequency without them is not a specification, and this exact conversation is why.
Problem. Why do IP vendors quote area in gate equivalents rather than square micrometres?
Solution. Because square micrometres are meaningless without naming the process, the library and the utilisation, and a vendor who names all three has effectively told a competitor which foundry and library they used. A gate equivalent — conventionally the area of a two-input NAND in the same library — is a ratio, so it survives the translation between processes approximately, and it lets a customer scale to their own library by multiplying by their NAND area. The approximation is worse than it looks, because the ratio of flop area to NAND area and of SRAM bit-cell area to NAND area both vary between libraries, so a flop-heavy block converts less accurately than a logic-heavy one. The honest datasheet therefore gives gate equivalents, flop count and memory bits separately, which lets the customer do the conversion properly, and costs the vendor nothing.
