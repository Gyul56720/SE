A design does not become a chip in one step. It passes through a chain of tools, each of which takes a description that is free in some respect and returns one that is not: synthesis fixes which gates exist, placement fixes where they are, CTS fixes when each one is clocked, routing fixes which metal carries which net. Each stage removes a degree of freedom, and no later stage can put it back cheaply. That single sentence explains the shape of the whole flow — why the effort is front-loaded, why the file formats look the way they do, and why the quantity a project manager actually tracks is not progress but iteration count.

T17.1 The chain, and what crosses each boundary

구현 흐름 (implementation flow) — The ordered chain of tools that turns an RTL description plus a set of constraints into manufacturable geometry. Ordered, because each stage's output is the next stage's input — and because the information each stage needs about the stages after it does not exist yet. That last clause is the source of every difficulty in this chapter.
The flow is a chain, but the information runs the other way. Synthesis must choose gate sizes to meet a delay it cannot know, because the delay depends on wire lengths that placement has not chosen yet. Placement must choose locations to meet a delay it cannot know, because routing has not happened. Every stage is therefore estimating its own successor, and the quality of that estimate — not the quality of the optimisation — decides how many times you go round.

A block met timing at every corner and failed in silicon at cold. The SDC had a set_false_path on a reset that was genuinely asynchronous on assertion — and synchronous on de-assertion (T3, X42). The false path removed the one check that would have caught the recovery violation. Nothing in the flow was wrong; the file that says what the flow should check was wrong, and it is the one file nobody generates automatically.

T17.2 The estimate that every stage makes about its successor

배선 부하 모델 (wire-load model) and why it was abandoned

Classical synthesis estimated a net's capacitance from its fanout using a table supplied with the library: a 4-fanout net in a block of this size has, statistically, this much wire on it. It is a defensible estimate when wire delay is a small part of the path.

It stopped being defensible for the reason computed in X14: wire delay does not scale with the transistors. Once the wire is a large fraction of the path, a statistical wire length is useless, because the paths that fail are exactly the ones whose wires are not typical. The estimate is right on average and wrong where it matters.

The replacement is physical-aware synthesis: synthesis runs a coarse placement internally, so its wire lengths come from positions rather than from a table. It does not make the estimate exact — it makes it wrong in the same direction as the next stage, which is what a usable estimate must be.

어디에 쓰나Every synthesis run of a block large enough that the longest wires cross a meaningful fraction of the die.
언제 쓰나Always, now. The wire-load flow survives only in very small blocks and in teaching material.
어떻게 쓰나Give synthesis the floorplan (die outline, macro positions, IO locations) before it optimises, not after. Then compare its predicted slack with post-route slack on the same paths — that difference is the number that tells you whether your flow converges.
실제 코드는 이렇게 생겼다
# The correlation check that belongs in every block's log. Not
# "did it meet timing" -- how far the estimate moved between stages.
#
#   path                 synth slack   place slack   route slack   drift
#   u_alu/…/sum[31]        +120 ps       +85 ps        -40 ps      -160 ps
#   u_ctl/…/state[2]        +95 ps       +90 ps        +70 ps       -25 ps
#
# A drift column that is large and negative means the flow is not converging:
# you are not closing timing, you are discovering it.
여기서 틀린다. A synthesis run that reports large positive slack on a physically aware flow is not good news by itself — check that the floorplan it used is the floorplan place-and-route will use. An optimistic estimate made against a stale floorplan is worse than no estimate, because it is believed.

T17.3 What a loop costs, measured

The flow's economics are not about tool quality. They are about where in the chain a mistake is found. A problem discovered at stage k requires re-running stages 1 through k, because each stage consumed the output of the one before it. The table below uses assumed runtimes for a one-million-instance block on a single server — assumed, and marked as such, because the real numbers depend on the tool, the machine and the design. What does not depend on those is the shape: the cost of a loop grows superlinearly with how late it closes, because the expensive stages are at the end.

Read the last row: a problem found in physical verification costs 51.2  h of compute to re-reach that point, against 0.2  h for the same problem found by lint — a factor of 256 ×. And that is only the machine time. The engineer's time, the calendar days, and the fact that the fix now has to be made without disturbing everything that already closed, are all on top of it.

This is the entire argument for front-loading. It is not a cultural preference for careful RTL; it is arithmetic. The stages are ordered cheap-to- expensive, so any check you can move earlier is multiplied by the ratio above. That is why lint, CDC analysis, formal equivalence and a synthesis run with a real floorplan are run constantly, and why 'we will fix it in the backend' is a decision to pay several hundred times more for the same fix.

T17.4 Why timing closure sometimes does not close

The loop cost above assumes you go round once. The harder question is how many times you go round — and there the flow has a property that surprises people the first time they meet it: closure can diverge. Fixing one violation changes the design, and a changed design can create violations that were not there before. Upsizing a cell to fix a setup path loads its driver; inserting a buffer takes placement space and pushes a neighbour; a hold fix adds delay a setup path was relying on not having.

유도 — Expected number of fixes when each fix can create another
이렇게 된다왜냐하면
1Start with one violation. Fixing it is one unit of work.That is the work we are counting — one ECO applied and re-analysed.
2Each fix creates a new violation with probability p, independently.This is the modelling assumption, and it is the one to argue with. It says a fix's side effects do not depend on how many fixes came before.
3The process is then a branching process with mean offspring p.One parent, p children on average — the standard Galton–Watson form.
4Generation n has expected size pn.Expectation is multiplicative across independent generations.
5Total expected work is 1 + p + p² + … = 1/(1−p) for p < 1.Geometric series. Its sum is the total number of fixes, not the number of rounds of the tool.
6For p ≥ 1 the series diverges: closure does not converge at all.Each fix creates at least one more on average, so the queue never empties. The design does not need a better engineer; it needs a change of floorplan, constraints or target frequency.

예제 3. Is this block going to close?
주어진 것. Round 1 of ECO fixed 400 violations and the next STA run reported 260 new ones that did not exist before. Round 2 fixed those 260 and produced 180 new.
푸는 법. Estimate p from the observed ratios: 260/400 = 0.65 and 180/260 ≈ 0.69. Take p ≈ 0.67. Expected total remaining work from the 180 now open is 180/(1−0.67).
답. About 545 more fixes — roughly three more rounds of the same size. It converges, slowly, if p stays at 0.67.
The independent check that matters here is not mathematical. Before believing p, confirm the new violations are genuinely new paths and not the same paths re-reported after a constraint change — a tool that re-reports is the trivial explanation for an apparent p, and it is measured by diffing the endpoint lists, not by looking at the counts.
여기서 틀린다. p is not a constant. It rises as utilisation rises and as the remaining violations get harder, so a p measured on the easy fixes underestimates the tail. Two consecutive rounds with a rising ratio is the signal to stop doing ECOs and change something structural — the arithmetic above says that once p reaches 1 no amount of ECO effort terminates.

특허로 가는 길 — 이 주제에서 발명이 일어나는 자리
The flow itself is not patentable, but two things inside it are and both are actively litigated: timing prediction before the geometry exists (estimating post-route delay from an RTL or netlist feature set, so the loop closes in minutes rather than days) and ECO placement — inserting fixes into a routed design without disturbing what already met timing. Both attack the same economics this chapter computes: the flow's cost is dominated by how many times you go round it.
