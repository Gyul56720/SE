T5. Sampling — Where a Continuous Signal Becomes a Number

Two clocks decide everything: when you sample, and how precisely you know when. The second one is what limits every high-speed converter shipping today.

Sampling is usually taught as an idealisation — multiply by an impulse train, observe the spectrum repeat, avoid overlap. That story is correct and it is not the one that limits hardware. A real sampler closes a switch for a finite time, through a finite resistance, at an instant known only to within some picoseconds, onto a capacitor whose size was already fixed by kT/C in T4. Each of those four words — finite, finite, instant, capacitor — costs resolution, and the one that dominates above a few hundred megahertz is the third.

T5.1 Aliasing is not a theorem you obey, it is a filter you buy

유도 — From the impulse train to the folded spectrum
이렇게 된다왜냐하면
1Sampling at fs multiplies the signal by a train of impulses spaced Ts = 1/fs.The idealisation. Everything about aliasing follows from this one step; the non-idealities come later and do not change it.
2Multiplication in time is convolution in frequency, and the impulse train's spectrum is itself an impulse train spaced fs.So the input spectrum is copied to every multiple of fs.
3Copies overlap unless the input is confined to a band narrower than fs/2.Nyquist. Note what it does not say: it says nothing about where the band sits, only how wide it is.
4Everything above fs/2 folds down and is indistinguishable from a real signal afterwards.No digital processing can undo it — the information that told the two apart was destroyed at the sampler. This is why an anti-alias filter is an analog component and cannot be moved into software.
5Noise folds too, and by the same rule.A front end with 5 GHz of bandwidth sampled at 200 MHz folds 50 bands of thermal noise onto the signal — a 17 dB penalty. Bandwidth you do not need is not free; it is noise you pay for.
Bandpass sampling — using the folding on purpose

Nyquist constrains the width of the band, not its position. A signal occupying 100 MHz centred at 2.4 GHz can be sampled at 250 MS/s, provided the band falls entirely inside one Nyquist zone and the sampler's input bandwidth reaches 2.4 GHz. The alias is the wanted signal, translated down for free.

The cost is precise: the sampling aperture must be good enough for 2.4 GHz even though the sample rate is 250 MHz — jitter is referred to the input frequency, not the clock rate. That trade (no mixer, but a far harder clock) is the whole engineering decision.

어디에 쓰나RF receivers that digitise at IF, software-defined radio front ends, and any instrument that must capture a narrow band sitting at a high frequency.
언제 쓰나When the band is narrow relative to its centre frequency and you would otherwise pay for a downconversion mixer and its LO.
어떻게 쓰나Pick fs so the band lands strictly inside a single zone (k·fs/2 to (k+1)·fs/2), add a bandpass anti-alias filter — a low-pass is useless here — and budget the jitter against the RF carrier.
실제 코드는 이렇게 생겼다
// Zone check, written as an assertion rather than a comment.
// A band that straddles a zone boundary is a silent failure: it still samples,
// and the output is two overlapped copies.
localparam real FS = 250e6, FC = 2.4e9, BW = 100e6;
initial begin
    int k = $floor(2.0*(FC - BW/2)/FS);
    assert ($floor(2.0*(FC + BW/2)/FS) == k)
        else $fatal(1, "band straddles a Nyquist zone boundary -- pick another FS");
end
여기서 틀린다. The anti-alias filter for bandpass sampling must reject every other zone, not just frequencies above fs/2. Engineers who reuse a low-pass filter here get a receiver that works in the lab (where the only signal present is the wanted one) and fails in the field.

T5.2 Aperture jitter — the wall

구경지터 (aperture jitter) — The rms uncertainty in the instant at which the sampler actually captures the signal. It comes from the clock path (phase noise, supply-induced delay modulation, thermal noise in the last buffer) and from the sampler itself, and it is referred to the input frequency, not the sample rate.
유도 — Why jitter costs more as the signal gets faster
이렇게 된다왜냐하면
1A sine v(t) = A·sin(2πfint) has slope dv/dt = 2πfinA·cos(…).The slope is what converts a time error into a voltage error — this is the whole mechanism.
2A timing error Δt therefore produces a voltage error Δv = (dv/dt)·Δt, worst at the zero crossing where the slope is greatest.The largest error happens where the signal is changing fastest, not where it is largest.
3With rms jitter σt and a full-scale sine, the rms error is 2πfin·(A/√2)·σt, and the signal rms is A/√2.The amplitude cancels — jitter-limited SNR does not depend on signal amplitude, which is why you cannot back away from it by scaling down.
4SNR = −20·log10(2πfinσt).One equation, two variables, no device parameters. It is a property of the clock and the input frequency alone.
5At fin = 1 GHz, 100 fs of jitter gives 64 dB — 10.3 bits.100 fs is already a hard clock. This is why 12-bit gigahertz converters are difficult in a way that has nothing to do with the comparator.

The specification that matters is the jitter at the sampling switch, after the whole distribution path — buffers, level shifters, the divider, and whatever supply noise the digital side injected into them. A 20 fs source behind a noisy buffer chain is a 200 fs sampler, and the datasheet of the source will not tell you that.
Jitter and kT/C add in power, not in amplitude, and they scale in opposite directions with frequency: kT/C is flat while jitter worsens by 6 dB per octave of input frequency. There is therefore always a crossover frequency, and knowing which side of it you are on tells you which knob does anything. Below it, spend area on capacitance. Above it, spend power on the clock — a bigger sampling capacitor buys nothing in a jitter-limited design, and it is the most common way to waste a redesign.

T5.3 The switch itself: charge injection, feedthrough, and bootstrapping

The bootstrapped switch

Hold the switch's gate at vin + VDD instead of at VDD, using a capacitor pre-charged to VDD and flipped onto the gate during the tracking phase. Then VGS is constant regardless of the signal, so Ron is constant and the charge injected at turn-off is constant — signal-dependent errors become offsets, and offsets are harmless in a differential design.

It also solves a reliability problem that is easy to miss: without bootstrapping, a switch passing a signal near ground sees VGS = VDD, which in an advanced node may exceed the oxide's rating over the device's lifetime (T14).

어디에 쓰나The input sampling network of essentially every SAR and pipeline ADC above 10 bits, and in high-linearity switched-capacitor filters.
언제 쓰나When THD or SFDR matters more than area. A plain NMOS switch is fine for 8 bits and hopeless at 12.
어떻게 쓰나Size the bootstrap capacitor ≫ the gate capacitance it must drive, check the reliability of every node across the clock phases, and verify distortion with a two-tone transient, not a single tone — intermodulation exposes the residual signal dependence a single tone hides.

Bootstrapping makes the gate voltage exceed the supply, so every node in the bootstrap network must be checked against the oxide limit at the fast, high-voltage, low-temperature corner — the opposite corner from the one that limits performance. A bootstrap circuit verified only at typical is a reliability report waiting to happen.

T5.4 Oversampling: buying bits with time

유도 — Why oversampling helps, and how much
이렇게 된다왜냐하면
1Quantisation noise power is LSB²/12 regardless of the sample rate.The quantiser does not know how fast it is being clocked; the error per sample has the same distribution.
2That fixed power is spread over the Nyquist band, 0 to fs/2.So the noise density is LSB²/12 divided by fs/2 — sampling faster dilutes it.
3Filtering down to the signal band B keeps only the fraction 2B/fs of it.Define the oversampling ratio OSR = fs/(2B).
4SNR improves by 10·log10(OSR): 3 dB, or half a bit, per doubling.This is plain oversampling, with no feedback. Going from 8 to 12 bits this way needs OSR = 256 — expensive but occasionally the right answer for a slow sensor.
5A ΔΣ modulator shapes the noise instead of merely spreading it, giving (6L+3) dB per doubling for an L-th-order loop.The loop pushes quantisation noise out of the band. A 2nd-order modulator gains 15 dB per octave of OSR — this is why ΔΣ dominates audio and precision sensing, and why it is useless at gigahertz signal bandwidths.

Oversampling relieves the anti-alias filter as much as it buys resolution, and that is often the real reason to do it. Sampling a 20 kHz audio band at 44.1 kHz demands a filter that is flat at 20 kHz and dead at 22.05 kHz — a brutal analog problem. Sampling at 2.8 MHz moves the requirement to 'dead at 1.4 MHz', which is a single RC. The filter that disappears is usually worth more than the half-bit.

The number to carry: at a 1 GHz input, 12 effective bits requires 38.9 fs rms of aperture jitter, measured at the switch and not at the clock source. Every architecture decision in a high-speed converter — interleaving, bandpass sampling, where the clock buffer sits — is downstream of that one line.

The next chapter turns the sampled voltage into a code, and finds that the architectures differ less in accuracy than in where they spend the same fundamental limits.

특허로 가는 길 — 이 주제에서 발명이 일어나는 자리
The sampling instant is the most contested square millimetre in a high-speed converter, and the patents show it: bootstrapped switches that hold VGS constant so the on-resistance does not depend on the signal; clock paths that deliberately do not share a buffer with anything else; sampling schemes that cancel charge injection by symmetry rather than by trimming; and, one level up, time-interleaved architectures whose entire difficulty is that each slice samples at a slightly wrong instant. Every one of these is an argument about the aperture, not about the amplifier.
