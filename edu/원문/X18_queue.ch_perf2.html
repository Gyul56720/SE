X18. Performance Modelling: Queues, Bursts and Tails

Most hardware performance questions are queueing questions in disguise: how deep must this FIFO be, how long will this request wait, what happens when two masters are busy at once. The answers are available in closed form for the simple cases and by simulation for the rest, and having both is what lets a designer say ‘sixteen entries’ with a reason attached.

X18.1 Little's law, which is almost too simple to be useful and is not

Little's law and its three uses
StepJustification
1In any stable system, L = λW: average number in the system equals arrival rate times average time in the system.It requires only stability — no distributional assumption whatever, which is why it is the most portable result in the subject.
2Use 1: from occupancy and rate, get latency.Measure the average FIFO occupancy and the throughput, and the latency comes out without instrumenting it.
3Use 2: from rate and latency, get the buffer needed.This is the bandwidth–delay product, which Parts X11, Y2 and Y3 each derived separately. They are the same theorem.
4Use 3: as a consistency check on a simulation.If measured L, λ and W do not satisfy it, the measurement is wrong — an independent cross-check of exactly the kind Part Y6 insists on, available free.

Deterministic service halves the queue, which is the quantitative reason fixed-size flits and fixed-latency pipelines are preferred in hardware: removing service-time variance is worth as much as halving the load.
Why hardware designers can be more aggressive about utilisation than the curve suggests, and where that reasoning fails. The classical curves assume Poisson arrivals, which model independent users. On-chip traffic is frequently self-limited: a master with four outstanding transactions cannot offer more than four, so the arrival process is closed rather than open and the queue cannot blow up — the right model is a closed network, and utilisation near one is achievable. The reasoning fails exactly when the traffic becomes open: a DMA engine with deep outstanding support, several masters that do not coordinate, or a packet interface fed from outside the chip. Ask whether the source can be throttled by the queue itself; if it can, be aggressive, and if it cannot, respect the pole.

Worked example 12. Sizing a FIFO from a burst specification rather than an average
Given. A source delivers a 64-byte burst at one byte per cycle, then idles for at least 192 cycles. The consumer drains at one byte every 2 cycles.
Method. Compute the accumulation during the burst and the drain afterwards; the peak occupancy is the answer, and it depends only on the burst, not on the average.

How this is got wrong. By computing the utilisation, seeing 0.5, and choosing a shallow FIFO. The system is only half loaded on average and still needs 32 bytes of storage, because the buffer's job is to absorb the difference between the arrival and service patterns, not to absorb the average. The general procedure is to integrate the difference between cumulative arrivals and cumulative service and take the maximum — a construction that generalises to any traffic pattern you can describe, and that is the basis of the network-calculus bounds used where a hard guarantee is required rather than an average.
Problem. Your interconnect meets its bandwidth requirement in simulation but a customer reports missed real-time deadlines. What is the likely disagreement?
Solution. You measured throughput and they need bounded latency, and the two are almost unrelated once utilisation is high. An arbiter that delivers full bandwidth can make one requester wait arbitrarily long — fixed priority does this by construction, as Part X11's measurement showed. The resolution has three parts. Establish which requester has the deadline and what it is, because a real-time requirement is a per-requester property and an aggregate number cannot express it. Measure the latency distribution, not the mean: report the 99.9th percentile and the maximum observed, since a deadline is violated by the tail. Then change the mechanism, not the tuning: a bandwidth regulator plus a priority class, or a reserved slot, gives a bound; adjusting weights gives an average. No amount of throughput tuning produces a latency guarantee, and recognising that these are different requirements early saves a redesign late.
