Z8. The Money

Part Y5 covers licensing structures. This part is narrower and more uncomfortable: what a one-person house's first three years look like as numbers, where the money actually goes, and which of the failure modes are arithmetic rather than bad luck.

Ranges, not quotations — they vary by country, body and year, and this repository has not obtained current price lists. The shape is what transfers: your time is the cost base and almost everything else is optional or small, with the two exceptions in bold.
Read the tools line against the standards line. The received wisdom is that EDA licences are what keep individuals out of chip design. For soft IP that is no longer true — Part Y14 measures a complete model-to-synthesis flow at zero — and the barrier has moved to a less discussed place: the specification. A protocol block you cannot legally read the standard for cannot be built at any price, and some bodies' membership is a five-figure annual commitment before a line of RTL exists. That is the real gate, and it is why Part Z5 weights ‘specification obtainable’ at 3 and why the post-quantum candidate scores so well: NIST published everything.

How this is got wrong. By budgeting the build and not the sale. The nine months are the part you control and the least of it; a customer's evaluation, legal review and purchase cycle is measured in quarters and does not start until the block exists. That gap is why Part Y8 recommends services first, and the recommendation is not about risk appetite — it is that the alternative requires two years of savings, and the failure mode is running out three weeks before the first sale, at which point the asset is worth nothing to you.

Do not discount to win the first deal without getting something back for it. The discount is defensible — a first customer takes a real risk on an unproven supplier and a public reference is worth more to you than the margin. What is not defensible is giving it away quietly: the price becomes the anchor for every subsequent negotiation, the customer's procurement remembers it, and you have bought nothing. Trade it explicitly: a named reference, a quotable result, an introduction, or a commitment to the second block in the family at full price. Write the exchange into the contract, because a verbal promise of a reference does not survive a change of contact.
Problem. A customer asks for source code escrow, unlimited indemnity, and a five-year support commitment. Which do you agree to?
Solution. Escrow: yes, and budget the annual fee. It is a reasonable request from anyone betting a product on a one-person supplier, and refusing it confirms exactly the risk they are worried about. Keep the deposit current — a stale escrow is worse than none, because it creates false comfort. Unlimited indemnity: no, and this is the one to hold. Cap it at fees received, which is standard and which a competent counterparty will accept; uncapped IP indemnity can exceed the value of everything you own, and no price compensates for that because the loss is unbounded. If they will not move, the deal is not worth doing at any price. Five-year support: yes, with the scope defined. Define what an update is — bug fixes in the delivered version, yes; a new revision of the standard, no, that is a new product — define a response time you can actually meet alone including holidays, and price the years beyond the first separately. The general rule is that the ones to resist are the unbounded ones, not the expensive ones; expensive is a number you can put in the quote, unbounded is not.
