Scores are judgements, and stating them as numbers is what makes them arguable. A reader who disagrees can change one cell and see whether the ranking moves — which is the point of a scorecard and the reason it beats a paragraph of reasoning.
On these weights the winner is ML-KEM (Kyber) NTT 코어, and the reason is worth reading off the table rather than taking on trust: it is the only candidate that scores 3 on all four of specification availability, independent golden model, soft RTL, and a changing standard with few suppliers. The post-quantum transition put a new, fully public, algorithmically demanding block into a market whose incumbents built their portfolios around RSA and ECC. And Part X6's entire FFT analysis transfers to the NTT — same radix choices, same twiddle symmetries, same memory-versus-multiplier trade, with modular arithmetic replacing complex arithmetic. A team that has built an FFT has most of it.
Every candidate scores zero on ‘a customer you can name’, and that is the real finding. The scorecard ranks technical and market fit; it cannot manufacture demand. Six good rows and a zero in that column describes a project, not a business. The action the table implies is not to start building the winner — it is to spend the next two weeks turning that column into a non-zero, by talking to people who buy this kind of block, and to let what they say re-score the other rows. A candidate that scores 40 with a named customer beats one that scores 48 without.

Z5.2 Sizing the winner before committing

Worked example 92. What an NTT core costs to build, estimated from the structure
Given. ML-KEM uses a 256-point NTT over the prime q = 3329, so coefficients are 12 bits and the modulus is small. Compare against the FFT arithmetic of Part X6.

How this is got wrong. By sizing the arithmetic and forgetting that the verification does not shrink with the datapath. The core is small; the golden model, the known-answer vectors from the standard, the constant-time argument (Part X36), the register map, the documentation and the release gate are the same work they would be for a block ten times larger. That asymmetry is good news for a first product — it means a small block can carry a full-quality wrapper, which is exactly what a first product needs to demonstrate.

No RTL in fourteen days, and that is deliberate. Every item above changes what the RTL should be, and each is cheap to redo and expensive to redo after the RTL exists.
Problem. You cannot get three customer conversations. Now what?
Solution. Treat that as data about the market rather than as an obstacle to route around. If nobody will spend twenty minutes discussing a block they might buy, the likeliest explanations are that the block is not a problem they have, that they already have a supplier they are satisfied with, or that you have not found the right people — and the third is the only one that is your mistake to fix. Widen the search before widening the scope: the people who answer are rarely procurement; they are engineers, and they are reachable through the standards body's working group, the open-source projects around the protocol, and conference and plugfest attendee lists. A useful substitute when conversations genuinely cannot be had is a public artefact that attracts them — the golden model published with the standard's vectors, a note on an ambiguity you found in the specification, a comparison of the open implementations. Part Y6's argument for publishing applies exactly here: it is how a house nobody has heard of becomes findable. What is not an answer is to start building and hope, because the 39 weeks are spent either way and only one of the two ends with someone to sell to.
