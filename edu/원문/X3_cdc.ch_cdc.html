X3. Metastability and Clock-Domain Crossing, Worked

A flip-flop sampled while its input is changing may enter a metastable state whose resolution time is unbounded. ‘Unbounded’ is the word that matters: there is no settling time that guarantees correctness, only a settling time that makes failure rare enough. Designing a synchroniser therefore means choosing a number of years, which is a much more concrete decision than it first sounds.

Worked example 38. Choosing the depth for a 1 GHz interface
Given. τ = 20 ps, T0 = 10 ps, fclk = 1 GHz, data edges at 100 MHz. Setup plus clock-to-Q consume 120 ps of each period.
Method. Compute MTBF for two and three stages, then compare against a target. A reasonable target for a part shipping in volume is not ‘longer than the product's life’ — it is longer than the product's life multiplied by the number of units in the field.
Resolution time, 2 stages1880 ps
MTBF, 2 stages1027.3 years
MTBF, 3 stages1049.0 years
2-stage MTBF across a 10-million-unit fleet1020.3 years
Same, but with τ doubled at the cold/low-V corner10-0.1 years
How this is got wrong. By evaluating the MTBF for one chip. The failure rate is additive over units, so a fleet of ten million divides the MTBF by ten million. A synchroniser that is comfortable in the lab can produce a field return every few weeks. The second common error is to use τ from a different library, or from a conference slide: τ varies by a factor of several between libraries and corners, and it sits in an exponent.
τ degrades at low voltage and cold, and the degradation is exponential in effect. A synchroniser characterised at nominal may have a τ 1.5–2× larger at the low-voltage corner; with tr/τ halved, an MTBF of 109 years becomes 104. Evaluate the MTBF at the worst corner for τ, not the typical one — and note that this is a corner the timing tool does not analyse for you, because the synchroniser path is one you told it to ignore.

X3.2 A synchroniser is not a CDC solution

Two flops make one bit safe. They do nothing for the problem that actually causes CDC bugs, which is that several bits crossing together may be sampled on different edges and produce a value that never existed in the source domain.

How this is got wrong. By assuming the synchroniser flops fix it. They do — per bit. The failure is a composition failure across bits, and no amount of depth on individual bits addresses it. The other frequent error is to Gray-code the pointer and then do arithmetic on it in the destination domain before converting back to binary; Gray codes do not add, and a comparison of Gray values is not a comparison of the numbers they encode except in the specific equality test that the standard FIFO empty/full logic uses.

The pulse-loss trap, stated precisely. A synchroniser samples; it does not detect edges. If the source asserts a signal for one source-clock cycle and the destination clock is slower, the destination may simply never see it. The rule is that a level crossing into a slower domain must be held for at least one full destination period plus the source's own uncertainty — in practice three destination cycles is the usual specification. When the ratio is not known at design time, because the customer sets both frequencies, a level crossing is unimplementable and the crossing must be a toggle or a handshake. This is a frequent finding in IP integration: the block worked at the vendor's ratio and fails at the customer's.
Problem. A configuration register is written in a 100 MHz APB domain and read by a 1 GHz datapath. The 32 bits change together. What is the correct structure, and what is wrong with synchronising each bit?
Solution. Synchronising each bit independently is wrong for the reason measured above: the datapath can observe a mixture of old and new fields. The standard structure is data hold with a synchronised enable: the APB domain writes the 32 bits into a holding register and then toggles a single control bit; that one bit crosses through a two-flop synchroniser; the datapath samples the 32 bits only when it sees the toggle. The data bits themselves never need synchronisers because they are guaranteed stable for several destination cycles before and after the sampling point — a guarantee that must be written into the constraint file as a set_max_delay -datapath_only, or the tool will either flag it or, worse, silently optimise the holding register away.
Problem. A reviewer says ‘add a third flop, it is nearly free’. When is that the wrong advice?
Solution. When the crossing's problem is not metastability. A third flop buys MTBF and costs one cycle of latency; if the observed bug is pulse loss, bus coherency or a missing constraint, the third flop changes nothing except the latency, and it can make a reconvergence problem worse by lengthening one path relative to another. Establish which of the four CDC failure modes you have before reaching for depth: metastability, pulse loss, incoherent multi-bit sampling, and reconvergence of separately synchronised signals. Only the first is helped by more stages.
