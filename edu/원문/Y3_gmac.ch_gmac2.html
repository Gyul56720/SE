Y3. The Ethernet MAC and Its Transceiver, Worked

The Gigabit MAC is the most-integrated communications block in existence and a good vehicle for the whole of this book: it has a datapath with hard real-time constraints, a protocol with a long tail of options, a clock-domain problem at its centre, and an interface stack whose names (GMII, RGMII, SGMII, QSGMII) encode a series of commercial compromises worth understanding.

How this is got wrong. By designing the datapath for aligned frames. Because the inter-packet gap is 12 bytes and the datapath is 8 wide, successive frames start at different byte lanes, so every parser, every CRC engine and every filter must handle all eight alignments. This is the single largest source of complexity in a wide Ethernet MAC and it is invisible in a specification that describes the protocol in bytes. A block that assumes alignment will pass a test bench that sends one frame at a time and fail on back-to-back traffic at line rate — which is the only traffic that matters.

The progression is one long trade of pins against signalling difficulty. RGMII halves GMII's pins by clocking on both edges and pays with a notorious source-synchronous timing problem: the specification's delay requirement is met either by the board, by the PHY or by the MAC, and mismatched assumptions between the three are the most common Gigabit Ethernet bring-up failure in existence.
The RGMII delay question, stated once so it can be checked. RGMII requires the clock to be delayed relative to the data by roughly 1.5–2 ns at the receiver. That delay may be provided by a long clock trace on the board, by the PHY's internal delay (enabled by strap or register), or by the MAC's output delay. Exactly one of the three must provide it. Zero gives a link that does not pass traffic or passes it only at 10 Mbit/s; two gives the same symptom for the opposite reason. Because each is configured by a different party — layout, PHY firmware, RTL — nobody owns the question, and it is worth writing the answer into the integration guide of any MAC a design house ships, along with the register bits that set it.

Where the design-house opportunity is in this stack. The PMA — serialiser, CDR, equaliser — is a hard macro and is not available to a soft-IP vendor. The MAC is a commodity: dozens of implementations exist, several of them open, and margins are thin. The PCS and FEC sublayer between them is soft RTL, is algorithmically demanding, changes with every new standard, and has far fewer credible suppliers. That combination — soft, hard, and changing — is the profile of a good product for a small house, and it is the reason this book's prior-art survey chose it. The specific opening at the time of writing is IEEE 802.3dj's concatenated FEC, where the outer RS-KP4 is well understood and the inner code and its interleaving are new.

The code appendix contains 260,596 lines of an open-source Ethernet family, of which 29,336 lines in 89 files are the BASE-R PCS itself — the 64b/66b encoder and decoder, the scrambler, the block synchroniser and the gearbox. Reading those files against the derivation above is the fastest way to see how a standard's prose becomes RTL.

Problem. A customer reports frame loss under burst traffic although your MAC reports no errors and the link is not saturated on average. Where do you look?
Solution. ‘Not saturated on average’ is the clue: averages hide bursts, and Ethernet traffic is bursty at every timescale. The sequence is: read the drop counters and find out which stage dropped — a MAC that cannot say whether a frame was dropped at the PHY interface, in the receive FIFO, or by the filter is much harder to defend, so this instrumentation is worth building. Then check the receive FIFO's depth against the consumer's worst-case stall, not its average service rate; if the consumer is a bus master, the stall is the interconnect's worst-case arbitration latency from Part X11, which is usually far longer than anyone estimated. Then check whether flow control is enabled at both ends, since PAUSE is negotiated and frequently disabled by default. Finally, check the clock-rate mismatch: two ends nominally at the same rate differ by up to 200 ppm, so a receiver without rate adaptation slowly falls behind and drops a frame every few seconds — a distinctive signature worth recognising, because the loss rate is remarkably constant.
Problem. Why does a MAC need an idle-insertion and idle-deletion mechanism at all, given that both ends are specified at the same rate?
Solution. Because ‘the same rate’ means the same nominal rate within a tolerance, typically ±100 ppm each, so the two can differ by 200 ppm — one part in five thousand. Over a continuous stream that is one extra or missing byte every 5000, which accumulates without bound. The mechanism uses the inter-packet gap as elastic: if the local clock is slower, delete an idle byte from the gap; if faster, insert one. The gap can be shortened to a floor (five bytes in the 10 Gbit/s specifications, from twelve) which is what limits how much rate difference can be absorbed. The consequence for a design is that the IPG a MAC sees on receive is not the IPG the far end transmitted, so any logic that assumes twelve bytes is wrong, and any logic that uses IPG timing to infer anything about the far end is wrong twice. This elasticity, and its floor, is also why the FEC and PCS layers above must tolerate the alignment markers that periodically consume the same budget.
