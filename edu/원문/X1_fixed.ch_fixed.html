X1. Fixed-Point Arithmetic, Worked End to End

Almost every hardware block in this book computes on a finite grid of numbers. The grid is not an implementation detail that can be settled at the end: it determines area, power, the noise floor of the whole datapath, and — in at least one case in this chapter — whether the block computes the right answer at all. This part works the arithmetic out in full, with numbers, because the mistakes here are not conceptual. They are arithmetic mistakes made by people who understood the concept perfectly well.

X1.1 The format and its four consequences

Write a signed two's-complement format as Q(I.F) with W = I + F + 1 bits in total, one of which is the sign. Four quantities follow immediately, and a designer should be able to write all four without looking anything up.

From the format to the four numbers that matter
StepJustification
1An integer n stored in W two's-complement bits satisfies −2W−1 ≤ n ≤ 2W−1−1.Definition of two's complement.
2The stored value is x = n · 2−F.That is what ‘F fraction bits’ means: a fixed scale factor that the hardware never represents.
3Hence resolution Δ = 2−F.Consecutive integers differ by one; multiply by the scale.
4Hence range is −2I ≤ x ≤ 2I − Δ.Substitute the integer bounds. Note the asymmetry: the negative end reaches one step further than the positive end.
5Dynamic range = 20 log10(2W−1) ≈ 6.02(W−1) dB.Ratio of the largest magnitude to the step size.
6Quantisation noise power = Δ2/12 for rounding, if the error is uniform on [−Δ/2, Δ/2].Variance of a uniform distribution. The ‘if’ is load-bearing and X1.4 measures a case where it fails.

How this is got wrong. By confusing the range with the resolution. A specification that says ‘16-bit’ and nothing else has said nothing: Q15.0 and Q0.15 are both 16 bits and they cannot hold each other's numbers. Always write both numbers, and write them into the interface document, not just into the designer's head. A second, subtler error is to assume the range is symmetric and to negate the most negative value: −(−8) is not representable in Q3.12 and will wrap or saturate.

X1.2 Rounding rules are not interchangeable

Three rounding rules appear in real RTL: truncation (drop the low bits, which rounds toward −∞ in two's complement), round-half-up (add half an LSB then truncate), and convergent or banker's rounding (round-half-to-even). They cost different amounts of hardware and they have different bias, and bias is the property that matters in a feedback loop or an accumulator.

How this is got wrong. By reasoning about the error variance and forgetting the mean. The random part grows as √N; the bias grows as N. At N = 4096 the DC offset is about 35× the noise, which in a DC-coupled path shows up as a fixed output error no amount of averaging removes. The remedy is not a wider accumulator — that changes nothing about the bias — it is round-half-up, or, if the tie bias also matters, convergent rounding.
When convergent rounding is worth its tie-detection logic. Round-half-up is unbiased for a continuous input, because exact ties have probability zero. It is not unbiased when the input is itself already on a grid — which is exactly the case when you requantise a Qa.b signal to Qa.b′ with b′ < b. Then ties occur with probability 2−(b−b′) and all of them round the same way. The table below measures precisely this case.

X1.3 Overflow: the one decision that is not a trade-off

Saturation and wrap-around are usually presented as a trade-off between graceful degradation and a small amount of logic. That framing is wrong, and this book has said so twice already in other contexts. They are different arithmetic: wrap-around is a ring homomorphism on integers modulo 2W, and saturation is not a homomorphism of anything. Where a design relies on the homomorphism, saturation breaks it. Where a design relies on a bounded output, wrap-around breaks that.

Why the CIC integrator must wrap
StepJustification
1A CIC decimator is N integrators, a downsampler, and N combs.Definition (Hogenauer).
2Integrator: y[n] = y[n−1] + x[n]. Comb: z[m] = y[m] − y[m−M].Definition.
3Work in ℤ2W. Addition and subtraction are well defined there and the map ℤ → ℤ2W commutes with both.That is exactly what ‘ring homomorphism’ asserts.
4Therefore the mod-2W image of the exact output equals the output computed entirely mod 2W.Homomorphisms commute with composition of ring operations; the whole CIC is such a composition.
5So if the final result fits in W bits, it is exactly right, however violently the integrators overflowed.The image determines the value once we know it is in range.
6Saturation is not such a map. sat(a+b) ≠ sat(sat(a)+b) in general.One counterexample suffices, and X1.3's worked example is one.

How this is got wrong. By choosing saturation as the ‘safe default’ in a coding standard. Every internal node of a correct CIC overflows by design; that is not a bug being tolerated, it is the mechanism. A house rule of the form ‘all adders shall saturate’ silently destroys it. Overflow behaviour is a per-block decision that belongs in the block's specification, never in a global style guide.
And the exact opposite is true one block away. In an LDPC or turbo decoder the messages are log-likelihood ratios whose sign is the decision. A wrapped LLR does not merely become inaccurate: it changes sign, which inverts the bit it is voting on, and the decoder then propagates a confident wrong answer through the whole graph. There saturation is mandatory and wrap is a catastrophic bug. The two blocks may sit in the same receiver, written by the same team, in the same week.

X1.4 When Δ2/12 is not the answer

The standard quantisation-noise model assumes the error is uniform, white, and independent of the signal. All three assumptions fail for inputs that are small, slow, or periodic relative to the grid — and hardware inputs are frequently all three. The next sweep measures the failure instead of asserting it.

What goes wrong below a few LSB, and the standard remedy. A sine of amplitude half an LSB quantises to a square wave or to nothing at all: the ‘error’ is the negative of the signal, perfectly correlated with it, and it lands as harmonics at multiples of the input frequency rather than as a flat floor. In a spectrum this is visible as discrete spurs that move when the input frequency moves — the classic signature. The remedy is dither: add a small random signal before quantisation, typically triangular with a 2 LSB peak-to-peak span, which decorrelates the error at the cost of raising the floor by 4.8 dB. Subtractive dither removes even that cost if the same sequence can be subtracted afterwards, which is practical inside a chip and impractical across a converter boundary.
Worked example 16. Dither converts a spur into a floor
Given. The 1.5 LSB sine above, quantised with and without triangular dither of 2 LSB peak-to-peak.
Method. Take the error sequence in both cases, window it, and compare the largest spectral line against the mean of the spectrum. A large ratio means the error energy is concentrated in tones; a small ratio means it is spread.

How this is got wrong. By reporting only the variance. Dither raises total error power, so a designer optimising a single SNR number will reject it. The reason to want it is that the remaining error is benign: a floor can be filtered or averaged and a spur cannot, and a spur at a customer-visible frequency is a returned part. Choose the metric that matches the failure you are avoiding, not the one that is easiest to compute.

X1.5 Word-length allocation as an engineering procedure

Problem. An 11-tap FIR must reach 70 dB SQNR. Multiplier cost is proportional to Fcoef × Fdata. Using the table above, which cell is cheapest?
Solution. Scan the table for cells at or above 70 dB and compare the products. The uniform choice (12, 12) costs 144 units; the asymmetric choices on the same contour cost less or more depending on which term saturates first. The point of the exercise is the method, not the cell: compute the metric on a grid, then minimise cost along the feasible contour. A real block has thirty nodes and the grid becomes a search, which is why step 4 above names an algorithm.
Problem. Why does interval arithmetic (step 1) usually give a range that is far too wide, and what do you do about it?
Solution. Interval arithmetic assumes every node simultaneously takes its worst value, which for an N-tap FIR means all inputs at full scale and aligned in sign with the coefficients — giving the ℓ1 bound Σ|hk|. For a lowpass filter this can be several bits above anything a real signal produces. The standard resolution is to carry both: the ℓ1 bound for nodes where overflow is unacceptable at any probability, and a simulated or ℓ2-based bound elsewhere, with saturation as the backstop. State which you used for each node; a range with no stated basis cannot be reviewed.
