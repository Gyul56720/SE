Z11. Writing the AXI Wrapper a Customer Will Accept

Almost every block a design house sells is bought as an AXI peripheral, and almost every integration complaint is about the wrapper rather than the core. This part is the wrapper — what it must do, what it must not do, and the specific mistakes that cost a week at the customer's end.

Z11.1 Keep the protocol out of the core

Why the boundary pays for itself three times
StepJustification
1A core that consumes and produces values, with a simple ready/valid or an enable, has no protocol in it.It can be tested against the golden model directly — Part Z1's arrangement.
2The wrapper translates AXI into that.And it is the same wrapper for every core you build.
3First payment: verification. The core is verified against the model; the wrapper is verified against the protocol.Two small problems instead of one large one, and the protocol half is reusable.
4Second payment: the customer. Some want AXI-Stream, some AXI4, some a bare FIFO, some APB for configuration only.A core with the protocol baked in has to be rewritten for each.
5Third payment: the standard changes and the core does not.AMBA revisions, a move to CHI, a customer's proprietary fabric — all wrapper work.
6The cost is one extra level of hierarchy and a handful of registers.Which Part X37's skid-buffer argument says you wanted anyway, because registering the boundary is what keeps your timing independent of their floorplan.

Six of the eight pass a functional test. They are found at integration, at line rate, or in the customer's system — which is why the release gate of Part Z3 checks throughput and why the testbench must apply randomised back pressure rather than accepting everything.
Worked example 72. Proving the wrapper does not halve your throughput
Given. An AXI-Stream wrapper around a core that accepts one word per cycle. The customer will measure sustained throughput with back pressure applied at random.
Method. State the bound, measure it, and put the measurement in the datasheet — the same three steps the agent's throughput check performs.
Ideal1 word/cycle
With random back pressure at 50 %0.5 word/cycle — expected
With a missing skid buffer0.25 — and every value is still correct
What a value-only regression sayspass
What the throughput check sayscycles > bound → red
Where the bound comes frommeasured on the clean block over several seeds
This repository's examplecrc32_stream: 2.18 clean vs 3.16 broken cycles/byte, bound 2.6
How this is got wrong. By declaring a bound from the architecture rather than from a measurement. The architectural ideal is one word per cycle and no real testbench achieves it, so a bound set from the ideal never fires and a bound set by guessing is either useless or a false alarm. Measure the clean block on several seeds, check the spread is small, and set the bound just above it — which in this repository's case caught a mutation that a value-only suite had missed.

The reference integration is the item with the best return and the one most often left out. It is an hour's work: your block, a minimal master that writes the registers and streams some data, and a script that runs it. What it removes is the first two days of every customer's evaluation, during which they are wiring your block up for the first time and every problem looks like your problem. A customer who runs your reference in ten minutes and sees traffic move has formed an opinion about your competence before reading the datasheet, and that opinion is expensive to change later in either direction.
Problem. The customer's interconnect is CHI, not AXI. How much work is that?
Solution. Ask first whether it has to be, because the answer is often no. Most CHI systems provide AXI bridges for peripherals, and a block that does not need coherence has no reason to speak CHI — so the first move is to establish whether the block is a coherent agent or a device behind a bridge, and for a datapath accelerator it is almost always the latter. If it genuinely must be a coherent agent, the work is substantial and is not a wrapper change: CHI is a coherence protocol with a snoop interface, ordering rules and a state machine, and Part X28's verification argument applies — the state space is the kind that needs formal methods, and the effort is comparable to the core itself. For a one-person house that is a reason to decline or to scope it as a separate project, not a line item in a wrapper quote. The middle case worth naming: some blocks need only IO-coherence, where the block's accesses snoop the host caches but nothing snoops the block. That is an interconnect feature, not a block feature, and it costs you nothing — which is worth knowing so you do not quote for work the customer's fabric already does.
