Y13. Getting Hired into a Modelling Team

This part is about the interview and the portfolio, treated as an engineering problem: what does the hiring side actually need to learn about you, and what evidence answers it fastest? The answer is unusually concrete for this kind of advice, because modelling work produces artefacts that can be shown.

Y13.1 What the interviewer is trying to find out

Table 391. The four questions behind a modelling interview
The questionHow it is usually askedWhat actually answers it
Can you write a correct model?A coding exercise, often a fixed-point or protocol taskCode that handles the corners and says which it handles
Do you know why the model exists?‘How would you verify this block?’An answer that mentions independence and what a disagreement means
Do you understand the hardware?‘Why is this architecture like this?’Reasoning from timing, area or bandwidth — numbers, not adjectives
Will you say when something is wrong?‘Tell me about a time…’, or a deliberately flawed premise in a questionNoticing the flaw and saying so, politely, is the whole test
The fourth question is weighted more heavily than candidates expect, and it is the one this book's discipline speaks to directly. A modelling engineer's value is that they are an independent check. An engineer who defers to the design team's reading of the specification is not performing the function they were hired for, however good their code is. Interviewers probe this deliberately — a question with a wrong assumption embedded, a requirement that contradicts an earlier one — and the answer they want is neither silence nor combativeness but the thing this book keeps recommending: name the discrepancy, cite the source, and ask.

Y13.2 A portfolio that is evidence rather than assertion

Table 392. Portfolio items, ordered by what they prove per hour of effort
ItemConcretelyWhat it proves
A bit-accurate model of a standard blockRS or BCH decoder, a CIC decimator, a 64b/66b PCSShows you can read a specification and produce something checkable
Its test suite, with mutation results‘These 14 injected faults; the suite catches 13; here is the escape and why’Almost nobody does this, and it is the single most convincing artefact
A word-length studyA sweep, a cost model, a chosen point with a reasonShows you understand the actual daily work
An RTL block compared against the modelEven a small one, with the comparison automatedShows you have closed the loop end to end
A written page on a bug you foundSymptom, hypotheses, the measurement that discriminated, the fixDemonstrates method, which is what is being hired
A contribution to an open projectA fix, a test, a documentation improvement in a real codebaseIndependent evidence that someone reviewed and merged your work
Depth beats breadth here. One block modelled properly, with its verification and its written reasoning, is worth more than six half-finished repositories — because the thing being assessed is whether you finish and whether you check, and only a finished, checked thing can show that.
Worked example 57. Where the hours go, and what each hour buys
Given. 120 available hours over three months, to be spent on a portfolio.
Method. Allocate against the four interview questions, weighting by how much evidence each hour produces.
Model of one standard block40 h

Total120 h
Questions from Y13.1 this evidencesall four
How this is got wrong. By spending all 120 hours on the model. A beautiful model with no test suite answers one of the four questions; the same model with 30 hours moved into verification answers three. The most common portfolio failure is an imbalance in exactly this direction, because modelling is the enjoyable part. The hours spent proving the model is right are the hours that differentiate, precisely because they are the ones most candidates skip.

Y13.3 Interview questions you should expect, and how to think about them

Table 393. Common questions and the shape of a good answer
QuestionWhat they want to hear
‘How do you know your model is right?’Independence of source; a third-party reference where one exists; property-based checks rather than sample values; fault injection to prove the checks bite
‘The RTL and your model disagree. What now?’Reproduce minimally, then go to the specification. Do not edit the model to match, and say so
‘How many bits does this filter need?’‘Against what metric?’ — then range, then sweep, then the knee. Asking for the metric first is most of the answer
‘Why not just use floating point?’Area, power and determinism; then the accumulation and associativity arguments from Part X9
‘Our coverage is 100 %. Are we done?’Code coverage measures execution, not checking. Offer mutation score and checker coverage as the evidence that would settle it
‘Tell me about a hard bug.’The method, not the heroics: hypotheses, the measurement that discriminated, what you changed in the process afterwards
One answer to avoid: ‘I would add more tests.’ It is the reflex response to every verification question and it is almost never the best one, because it does not say which tests or how you would know they were enough. The answers that distinguish a candidate name a measurement: mutation score, checker coverage, a comparison against an independent reference, a property that holds for all inputs rather than a vector that holds for one. Every one of those is something this book has computed somewhere, and being able to say ‘I would measure X and here is what X would tell us’ is the difference between sounding careful and being useful.
Problem. You are asked to estimate something you do not know — how many gates a block needs, how long a task takes. What do you do?
Solution. Estimate out loud, from decomposition, and say what would sharpen it. The interviewer is not testing recall of a number; they are watching whether you can bound an unknown, which is most of engineering judgement. A good answer names the decomposition (‘the multiplier array is about n² full adders, a full adder is about six gates, so…’), states the assumption that matters most, gives a range rather than a point, and ends with the measurement that would collapse the range. Refusing to estimate is the worst answer and a confident unqualified number is the second worst; the habit being looked for is the one Part X1 applies to word lengths and Part Y8 to schedules — a bound, its basis, and what would improve it.
Problem. You have no industry experience and no degree from a famous university. What actually compensates?
Solution. Artefacts, and one specific kind of them. A repository containing a model, its test suite, a mutation measurement of that suite, and a written page explaining a bug it caught is evidence that nobody can produce without having done the work — and it is checkable in ten minutes by the person deciding whether to interview you. Two things amplify it. Contributions to a real open project carry an independent review signal: someone with no reason to flatter you merged your change. And a written explanation of something difficult — a blog post deriving a result, a clear note on a standard's ambiguity — demonstrates the communication half of the job, which is half of it. What does not compensate is a list of technologies; every candidate has one, and it distinguishes nobody. Build one thing completely and write down how you know it works.
