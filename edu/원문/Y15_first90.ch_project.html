Y15. A Complete Worked Project: RS-KP4

This part takes one block from a specification through to a delivery package, with the numbers computed rather than estimated. It uses the code that is already in this repository — the field arithmetic and the decoder of Part X5 — and it states, at each step, what exists and what does not, so the reader can see the difference between a worked example and a finished product.

Three of six are assumptions. The model in this repository takes all three as arguments with the assumption marked, and its tests check properties that hold whatever the values are — that up to t errors are always corrected and that beyond t the decoder does not silently return the input. When the clause is obtained, only the defaults change.
This is what it looks like to build honestly on an unread specification. The alternative — pick a polynomial, hard-code it, and proceed — produces a block that works perfectly against itself and fails at the first interoperability test, with the cause buried in a constant nobody questions. Parameterising costs nothing and the marked confidence costs one column in a table. What it buys is that the unread clause remains visible instead of dissolving into an assumption, which is precisely the failure Part Y6 is written to prevent.

The third column is the block's fundamental parallelism: at 830 MHz the decoder must absorb 64 symbols every cycle. Every stage of the architecture must be replicated or restructured to that width, and that replication, not the algorithm, is where the area goes.

How this is got wrong. By optimising Berlekamp–Massey. It is the algorithmically interesting stage, it is what the literature discusses, and here it is about one per cent of the multipliers. The syndrome and Chien stages dominate because they are replicated 64 ways and BM is not. The architectural leverage is therefore in sharing and folding those wide stages — the standard techniques being common-subexpression sharing across the syndrome constants and a folded Chien search — and in nothing else. Compute the replication factor before deciding what to optimise, in any block.

Listing what does not exist is the point of this section. A worked example that presents a partial project as a finished one teaches the wrong lesson twice: it misleads about the effort, and it models the behaviour — quiet omission of the incomplete parts — that this book spends two chapters warning against. The honest summary is that this repository contains a verified reference model and one verified RTL primitive for a block whose full implementation is most of Part Y8's 39-week plan, and that three of its parameters await a document that must be purchased.
Problem. Given that state, what are the next three actions, in order?
Solution. First, obtain the clause. Three unconfirmed constants block every downstream claim of interoperability, and no amount of RTL removes that block; until they are confirmed the project can produce a correct decoder for a code that may not be the standard's. This is a purchase and a fortnight of reading, and it is the highest-value action available. Second, build the syndrome stage at full parallelism and verify it against the model, because the sizing example above says that stage dominates the area, so the area and frequency claims — the numbers a customer asks for first — are determined by it. One wide stage, synthesised and timed, converts an estimate into a measurement. Third, find a customer conversation before writing the key equation, because everything after the syndrome stage is a large effort whose requirements (latency budget, code rate set, concatenation) the customer determines, and guessing them is how the 39 weeks turn into 60. Note that the technically interesting work is third on this list, which is usually where it belongs.
