Z1. How a Chip Is Described: Levels of Abstraction
This chapter is the gentlest in the book and the one that everything else refers back to. A modern chip contains on the order of
1010 transistors; no person reasons about them individually. The field copes by stacking abstractions, each hiding the one be‐
neath. Knowing the stack — and knowing where it leaks — is the organising skill of the discipline.

Undergraduate review ·The middle of this stack is where digital IP work happens. A digital designer writes RTL and trusts
the synthesis tool to produce gates, the library to characterise them, and the process to build them. The trust is well-founded
almost all of the time.
Graduate ·Almost all of the time is the important qualifier, and the exceptions form a checklist worth memorising.  Ab‐
stractions leak in a small number of recurring ways, each of which appears later in this book: 
Timing — RTL describes behaviour without delay, but a real gate takes time, so a functionally correct design can fail
(Chapter B3).
Metastability — a flip-flop is modelled as producing 0 or 1, but a real one can do neither for a while (Chapter C3).
Power — RTL has no notion of energy, yet switching activity and glitching are decided by RTL structure (Chapter B2).
Physical size — RTL has no geometry, but wire delay depends on it (Chapter B4).
Side channels — two circuits computing the same function can be distinguished by their power consumption (Chapter
H3).
Radiation and ageing — a stored bit is assumed permanent; physically it is not (Chapter K3).
A useful way to read the rest of this book is as a systematic tour of these leaks , because they are exactly the places where
an engineer's judgement is still required and a tool cannot be trusted alone.

The cost of fixing a defect rises by roughly an order of magnitude at each phase boundary.  A specification ambiguity
caught while writing the specification costs an hour. Caught in verification it costs a week. Caught at bring-up it costs a mask
set — hundreds of thousands to millions of dollars and a three-month schedule slip. This asymmetry, not perfectionism, is
why the industry invests so heavily in front-end rigour, and it is the economic reason a modelling team exists at all.
